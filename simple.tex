\section{Introduction}
Observing subjects perform acts of choice is the basis of much work in econometrics. Such acts have been used, among others, to give an observable basis to the concept of preference \citep{samuelson_foundations_1983}. %Acts of choice are considered here in the broad sense of choice between courses of actions, encompassing the simple setting of choosing between various elementary consumption bundles as well as settings involving more abstract acts such as choosing a strategy in a game-theoretical setting. 

Most of these approaches consider only acts of choice that are performed spontaneously in the normal course of action of an individual, or that are given a specific frame depending on the psychological aspect that is to be studied. As an example of the former kind, typical willingness to pay studies asks the subject whether she prefers an object at some price or another object at another price, describing the objects in a way that is considered immediately understandable by the subject (or simply showing the objects). Examples of the latter kind include the celebrated work of Kahneman and Tversky \citep{bell_descriptive_1988, kahneman_thinking_2012}.

On the other hand, since at least the seminal work of \citet{fishkin_when_2011}, another sort of judgment involving subjective desirability is emerging as also worth studying. These are judgments given by subjects after arguments in favor and against different possible stance have been considered. Such judgments may differ from immediate judgments of desirability given in natural conditions. Indeed, individuals may change opinion on the desirability of some course of action, and therefore revise their choice, while learning about the properties of objects, the consequences of some acts (or empirical knowledge useful to estimate the likelihood of some consequences), or the logical or empirical impossibilities between consequences. This is particularly likely to happen when choosing among non everyday objects or acts whose consequences are not well known by the subject.

While numerous articles have discussed the concept of deliberation in the last decades, it remains unsettled, to the best of my knowledge, how to observe a deliberated judgment in a systematic and reproducible fashion. There is a need to define this concept sufficiently precisely that, first, it is clear what counts as a deliberated judgment (i.e., when “sufficient” \citep{meinard_justification_2020} or “correct” deliberation has happened); and second, it is clear which part of the claim is empirical and which part stems from an unfalsifiable conception of what “deliberated” means. This permits to study deliberated judgments empirically, meaning here that disagreements about someone’s deliberated judgment in a given context can be solved, at least in principle, by an empirical experiment, provided the very conception of what constitutes a deliberated judgment is shared. This article is interested in defining the concept of deliberated judgement of a subject and show how one can study it in an empirical manner. The “classical” counterpart of deliberated judgments, that does not involve confrontation with arguments, will henceforth be called the natural judgment. As we deal with choice acts, we will also name the concept “deliberated choice”, as opposed to “natural choice”.

While the concept of deliberation in the deliberative democracy literature usually refers to multiple individuals debating, this article considers deliberation as related to a single individual: an individual deliberates (in the sense of: carefully weights) the stance she considers most appropriate while being confronted to arguments. The deliberated judgment of an individual is thus defined with no requirement that the arguments be uttered by peers in a debate; they can be given by an automaton, for example. In this sense, deliberated judgments come close to what \citet{rawls_theory_1999} calls reflective equilibrium (referring to an idea of \citet{goodman_fact_1983}), a concept that has much inspired the present proposal.

To define deliberated judgments, one needs to start with the set of arguments under consideration, and the way the subject gets to know them, called hereafter the “exposure protocol”. As a result, we need always talk about the deliberated judgment \emph{given some exposure protocol}. The notion of argument invoked here is an extremely large one, including texts, images, sounds, experiences (a hiking trip), and so on: anything that can be transmitted to a subject qualifies as a possible argument. In particular, such an “argument” need not have the logical structure of what is considered a proper argument in argumentation theory. This important feature of this proposal permits to avoid taking a position on what is a correct argument, and permits to study wide enough conceptions of deliberated judgment, including those involving no paternalism \citep{cailloux_formal_2020}: anything that influences the judgment of a subject, as judged by the subject herself and not by any external standard, may a priori be considered worth including in at least some conceptions of deliberated judgment, thus including whatever some could consider as “incorrect reasoning”, “fallacies” or “bad reasons”.

The dependence of the deliberated judgment concept on the exposure protocol yields a first qualification about the concept of deliberated judgments when one desires to use it normatively: it is only as reasonable to call the resulting judgments “deliberate” in a normative sense as the exposure protocol is normatively relevant to deliberation. A sufficiently restricted, or even purposefully chosen, set of arguments may yield judgments that are only deliberate in the technical sense used here, but that pertain more to brain-washing than to reflective judgments. While it is indeed a long-term goal of this research program to yield a normatively relevant conception of preference (appropriate, for example, for recommendation), it is not a claim of this article that anything that corresponds to deliberated judgments as defined here qualify as being appropriate for any normative use: much depends on the chosen exposure protocol. The goal of this article is rather one of a conceptual and practical clarification. It proposes a sharp distinction between the empirical content and the normative content of the concept of deliberated judgments: the definition proposed here transfers the full normative weight on the choice of the exposure protocol; and leaves the rest of the disagreements to empirical settling. This is methodologically akin, mutatis mutandis, to the axiomatic study of justice such as done in social choice, only complementing mathematical deduction with empirical claims. In social choice, the choice of axioms bears the full normative weight; once axioms are chosen, it is a matter of logic which social choice rules (if any) correspond to that choice. In our case, once an exposure protocol is chosen, it is an empirical matter which deliberated judgments result.
In supplement to a possibly helpful conceptual clarification, such a sharp separation may reveal practically useful for separation of concern as it permits to study deliberated judgments empirically under various exposure protocols independently of possible disagreements about which norms are more appropriate for deliberation in which circumstances.

The deliberated judgment of a subject is said to depend on the exposure protocol, that is, on the set of arguments and the way they are submitted to the subject, and not only on the set of arguments, because the very way that they are transmitted to the individual may affect her judgment differently. For example, reading an image description and showing an image to a subject may impress them differently \citep{railton_facts_2003}.

The exposure protocol defines a set of argument and, importantly, how arguments are sent to the subject and her subsequent choice observed. There is no explicit dependency on the internal process that occurs in the subject’s mind while she (possibly) processes the arguments, as it is not desirable to assume that the internal process could be observed. We consider subjects as black boxes and only require a capacity of observation of their choices after exposure to arguments. Depending on the exposure protocol and the subject, it is of course possible for the arguments to never affect any choice of the subject, a conclusion that may be interesting in its own right.

The deliberated judgment of a subject is defined as a function of the exposure protocol, as accepting every claims that resists every counter-arguments. The notion of resistance is itself defined according to the exposure protocol.

Studying judgments resulting from exposure to arguments includes a difficulty that is not present with natural choice experiments: exposing an individual to arguments may change the individual’s stance, an effect that cannot be erased in order to submit the individual to an unrelated sequence of arguments. The observer thus cannot in general assume that multiple unrelated sequences of arguments can be tested on a given individual. This difficulty will be worked around by considering general theories of deliberated judgments, that apply to sets of individuals. This is methodologically similar to defining theories of how objects break. One can break a given (unrepairable) object only once, so such theories must apply to sets of objects if one wants to be able to test multiple ways of falsifying the theories.

For conceptual clarity, I will focus on the case of a single binary decision to take, akin to a choice situation facing a bundle of two courses of action, exactly one of which must be adopted.

\section{Exposure protocol and deliberated choice}
I assume given two antagonistic acts, constituting the decision at stake, such as “buy this object for the price of \$15” and “do not buy this object for the price of \$15”, or “take this job offer” and “decline this job offer”.
%The letter $φ$ will be used to denote a generic act (one of these two), and given an act $φ$, $φ'$ denotes the other act.
Let the set $P = \set{φ, φ', 0}$ denote the set of possible preferences, where $φ$ denote a strict preference for the first act against the other one, $φ'$ conversely, and $0$ denotes indifference (both acts are considered equally worth).

\subsection{Exposure protocol}
An exposure protocol is a tuple $(I, \allargs, {\allleadsto})$, where $I$ is a set of individuals, $\allargs$ is a set of arguments, and ${\allleadsto}$, “leads to”, is the behavior of the individuals as a function of the arguments they have been exposed to.

While $I$ and $\allargs$ are fundamental sets whose elements are considered elementary, a few notations and concepts are required to define ${\allleadsto}$.
Throughout the article, $ℕ$ designates the natural numbers (including $0$) and $\Np = ℕ ∖ \set{0}$ designates the positive numbers.
Given $j, l ∈ ℤ$, the notation $\intvl{j, l}$ represents $\set{k ∈ ℤ \suchthat j ≤ k ≤ l}$ (thus $∀ j ∈ \Np: \intvl{j, 0} = ∅$).
Define $\allhists = ⋃_{k ∈ ℕ} \allargs^{\intvl{1, k}}$ as the finite sequences of arguments, including the empty sequence.
A generic element of $\allhists$ (a possibly empty finite sequence of arguments) is denoted by $\hist$ and called an argumentative path, or simply a path. Its length is denoted by $\card{\hist} ∈ ℕ$ and its elements, $\hist(k)$ or $\hist_k$.
Thus, given an argumentative path $\hist ∈ \allhists$, $∀ k ∈ \intvl{1, \card{\hist}}: \hist(k) = \hist_k ∈ \allargs$, and $\hist = ∅ ⇔ \card{\hist} = 0$. 
Throughout the text, functions are set-theoretically considered as sets of pairs (identified with their graph), so that for example a path is seen as a subset of $ℕ × \allargs$.
A path $\hist ∈ \allhists$ is a prefix of a path $\histp ∈ \allhists$ whenever $\hist ⊆ \histp$, thus, $∀ k ∈ \intvl{1, \card{\hist}}: \hist_k = \histp_k$.
Given any $\hist ∈ \allhists$, $\intvl{\hist} = \set{\restr{\hist}{\intvl{1, k}} \suchthat k ∈ \intvl{0, \card{\hist}}}$ denotes the set of prefixes of $\hist$.

The function ${\allleadsto}: I × \allhists → P$ associates to each individual and path an element of $P$, ${\allleadsto}(i, α)$ representing the choice of $i$ following exposure to the arguments of $α$, in order. That is, ${\allleadsto}(i, α) = φ$ denotes that $i$ chooses the first act after exposure to $α$, ${\allleadsto}(i, α) = φ'$ denotes $i$ choosing the second act and ${\allleadsto}(i, α) = 0$ is used to represent indifference of $i$.
The notation $α \ileadsto p$, pronounced “$α$ leads $i$ to $p$”, will be used to mean ${\allleadsto}(i, α) = p$, considering ${\ileadsto} ⊆ \allhists × P$ as a binary relation.
As usual with binary relations, $\hist \nileadsto p$ means ${\allleadsto}(i, α) ≠ p$ or equivalently ${\allleadsto}(i, α) ∈ P ∖ \set{p}$.

\subsection{Deliberated choice}
The deliberated choice of $i$ given the exposure protocol $(I, \allargs, {\allleadsto})$ is denoted $P_i ⊆ P$.
Its formal definition requires the notion of decisive argument.

Given $\hist ∈ \allhists$, $\range(\hist) = \hist(\intvl{1, \card{\hist}})$ denotes the range of $\hist$, that is, all arguments contained in the sequence $\hist$.
Let $\histend = \hist(\intvl{\max(1, \card{\hist} - 1), \card{\hist}})$ denote the set containing the last two arguments of the sequence $\hist$ if the sequence has at least two elements; the unique argument of the sequence if it has exactly one element; and the empty set if it is empty.

Given $i ∈ I, p ∈ P, \ar ∈ \allargs$, define $\ar \ileadstost p$, pronounced “$a$ leads definitely $i$ to $p$”, iff $∀ \hist ∈ \allhists \suchthat \ar ∈ \histend: \hist \ileadsto p$.
When $\ar \ileadstost p$, $\ar$ is said to be a decisive argument for $p$ from $i$’s point of view given the exposure protocol.
Another interpretation of this definition is that $\ar$ then resists every counter-arguments, in the sense that no argument $\ar'$ given at the end of any sequence of arguments, in presence of $\ar$, leads the individual to $p' ≠ p$ (formally, $∄\ar' ∈ \allargs, \hist ∈ \allhists \suchthat \ar, \ar' ∈ \histend ∧ \hist \nileadsto p$).

The deliberated choice of $i$ is then considered as the set of propositions supported by decisive arguments.
\begin{definition}[Deliberated choice]
  \label{def:decisive}
  $∀ i ∈ I, p ∈ P:
    p ∈ P_i ⇔ [∃ \ar ∈ \allargs \suchthat \ar \ileadstost p].$
\end{definition}

It follows from the consideration of “end” as the last \emph{two} arguments that the whole set of decisive arguments can argue for at most a single proposition, thus, that the deliberated choice cannot hold more than one proposition.
\begin{theorem}[Deliberated choice singularity]
  \label{th:sing}
  $∀ i ∈ I: \card{P_i} ≤ 1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  Assume that $∃ \ar, \ar' ∈ \allargs, p, p' ∈ P \suchthat \ar \ileadstost p ∧ \ar' \ileadstost p'$.
  Since $\ar \ileadstost p$, $(\ar, \ar') \ileadsto p$, and since $\ar' \ileadstost p'$, $(\ar, \ar') \ileadsto p'$, hence $p' = p$.
\end{proof}

The intended semantics is that $P_i$ represents the preference of $i$ over the proposed set of two acts, after deliberation, given the exposure protocol. In other words, if $P_i = \set{φ}$, the individual considers the first act strictly better than the second one, after deliberation (and conversely for $P_i = \set{φ'}$); if $P_i = \set{0}$, the deliberated judgment of $i$ is that both acts are equally worth; and if $P_i = ∅$, no act is deliberately strictly better than the other one and they are also not deliberately exactly equivalent to each other.

This latter possibility may look unpleasant, but it is a fact that facing some difficult problems, individuals sometimes are undecided, and it is an a priori empirical possibility that the individual remains undecided (or even, \emph{becomes} undecided) when considering multiple arguments.
Being undecided is not the same state as being decidedly indifferent about two issues, and those states can be distinguished empirically, as we will see.

Given an behavior function $\allleadstog$, deliberated choices are represented by an element $π({\allleadstog}) ⊆ I × P$ defined as $π_i(\allleadstog) = π({\allleadstog})(i) = P_i$. \Cref{th:sing} shows that $π({\allleadstog})$ is right unique (meaning that $∀ i ∈ I: \card{π({\allleadstog})(i)} ≤ 1$).
Equivalently, note that $(P ∪ \set{∅})^I$, where $\set{∅}$ is used as a sentinel value for individuals with no deliberated judgment, can be interpreted as the right unique relations over $I × P$ (discarding those individuals with no deliberated judgment); and then the deliberated choices are an element $π({\allleadstog}) ∈ (P ∪ \set{∅})^I$.

\subsection{An example and a discussion about observability}
The following toy example is inspired by the following argument from \citet[p.~59]{railton_facts_2003}.
\begin{quote}
  An experiment in cognitive psychology reveals that when subjects observe two individuals taking a test, one of whom answers a high percentage of the early questions correctly but then falters, while the other does
  poorly initially but then answers a high percentage of the later questions
  correctly, the first is usually viewed as more able, even though in the end
  each answers the same number of questions correctly in all. If the subjects had been asked which test-taker they would prefer as a math tutor,
  they likely would have answered “The first,” and perhaps they would also
  have been willing to pay a premium to secure his services. But if they
  were to become convinced of the influence of sheer order on their evaluations of relative ability, they presumably would no longer want their
  initial preference for the first test-taker to influence their choices – or
  willingness to pay – in this way.
\end{quote}
\begin{example}[Math tutor]
  \label{ex:tutor}
  Let $I$ be a set of individuals desiring to evaluate two candidates $\set{m_1, m_2}$ for math tutoring.
  Consider one individual $i ∈ I$.
  Let ${\allleadsto}(i, a_1)$ represent $i$ being shown a test of candidate $m_1$ wherein $m_1$ answers 70\% of the first half of questions correctly but then only 40\% of the other half correctly.
  Let ${\allleadsto}(i, a_2)$ represent $i$ being shown a test of candidate $m_2$ wherein $m_2$ answers 45\% of the first half of questions correctly, then 75\% of the other half correctly.
  %	Let ${\allleadsto}(i, a_e)$ represent $i$ being given an explanation about the psychological phenomenon described in the quote above.
  Let ${\allleadsto}(i, a_e)$ represent $i$ being given the following argument. “When tested, $m_1$ answers 70\% of the first half of questions correctly but then only 40\% of the other half correctly; whereas $m_2$ answers 45\% of the first half of questions correctly, then 75\% of the other half correctly. It follows than $m_1$ answers on average less well than $m_2$. Observing the candidates take the tests may yield a contrary impression; this is a well-known psychological phenomenon depending on the order of the questions that the candidates answer correctly.”
  Let $φ_1$ represent choosing $m_1$ and $φ_2$ choosing $m_2$ (for this example we use $P = \set{φ_1, φ_2, 0}$).

  If the prediction described in the quote applies to $i$, the relation $\ileadsto$ satisfies, $∀ k ∈ \Np$ (recall that for $\args ⊆ \allargs$, $\args^k$ represents the set of sequences of length $k$ using arguments from $\args$): 
  $∀ \hist ∈ \set{a_1, a_2}^k, \hist \ileadsto φ_1$ and
  $∀ \hist ∈ \allargs^k \suchthat a_e ∈ \histend, \hist \ileadsto φ_2$.
  The latter in particular implies:
  $∀ \hist ∈ \set{a_e}^k, \hist \ileadsto φ_2$; 
  $∀ \hist ∈ \set{a_1, a_e}^k \suchthat a_e ∈ \histend, \hist \ileadsto φ_2$; 
  $∀ \hist ∈ \set{a_2, a_e}^k \suchthat a_e ∈ \histend, \hist \ileadsto φ_2$.
  The values of ${\allleadsto}(i, \hist)$ for $\hist = ∅$ and for $\hist ∈ \allargs^k \suchthat a_e ∉ \histend$ are undetermined by the prediction, but whatever they are, $a_e$ is a decisive argument for $φ_2$, hence, $i$’s deliberated choice is $φ_2$.
\end{example}

Now that deliberated choices are defined, we need to explore what one can learn about deliberated choices using our empirically available data.
This requires to define claims about deliberated choices and to explore how to validate claims.

\section{Claims about deliberated choice}
Given an exposure protocol $(I, \allargs, {\allleadsto})$, consider a claim about $\allleadsto$, such as “every individuals in $I$ choose deliberately $φ$”, or, in \cref{ex:tutor}, “$a_e$ is a decisive argument for $i$ in favor of $φ_2$”.
Validating claims about deliberated choice is no trivial matter, for two reasons.

First,
such claims must entail an infinity of constraints about $\allleadsto$:
in order to deduce that $p ∈ P_i$ for some $p ∈ P, i ∈ I$, the claim must constrain $\ileadsto$ on every possible paths $α ∈ \allhists$.
It follows that a claim cannot be validated empirically exhaustively by observing the value of $\allleadsto$ on every points that it constraints.

Second,
our observations are limited in important ways: once we have shown some arguments to a given individual $i$, we cannot undo the effects of this action on $i$ and show to her a different argument as if starting from scratch (unless assuming $i$ forgets arguments after a while and waiting for sufficiently long, which would create practical and theoretical difficulties).
In other words, once we have shown a path $\hist$ to $i$, we can only continue the protocol with $i$ by showing paths $\histp$ that extend $\hist$.

To illustrate this point with \cref{ex:tutor}, let $\hist = (a_1, a_e, a_2)$ designate the argumentative path where the arguments are shown in the order they are described in \cref{ex:tutor}, and let $\hist' = (a_2, a_e, a_1)$ designate the reverse order.
The deliberated choice concept is defined so as to not depend on the order of the arguments shown to $i$, as long as the end of the path features a decisive argument: for having $p ∈ P_i$, it must be that directing to $i$ the path $\hist$ or the path $\hist'$ both lead $i$ to choose $p$.
But it is impossible to observe both the value of ${\allleadsto}(i, \hist)$ and the value of ${\allleadsto}(i, \hist')$.

The first difficulty is familiar as it occurs in any endeavor to validate general claims in science: claims that cover a large (or infinite) spectrum cannot be validated by observing the system exhaustively. One must rather develop a falsification protocol, that is, a way to invalidate the claim, and then observe that the claim is not invalidated. This is the approach taken in the next section.

This approach also permits to tackle the second difficulty, by letting us study theoretically the needs and limits of falsification given our observable basis.
% Such theories contain an observable claim and a conclusion claim, so as to satisfy two properties. First, such a theory must be falsifiable in principle, meaning that it must be possible, using our observational basis (propositions of the sort $α \ileadsto φ$), to invalidate the observable claim of the theory. As a counter-example, using \cref{ex:tutor}, a theory that claims that $∀ i ∈ I: α_{123} \ileadsto \prop_1 ∨ α_{321} \ileadsto \prop_1$ is not falsifiable in principle, as, whatever the real $\ileadsto$ relation, with a given $i$, one can never show that $α_{123} \nileadsto \prop_1 ∧ α_{123} \nileadsto \prop_1$. Second, such a theory must be so that if its observable claim holds, then some given alternative is in the deliberated judgment of the individuals in $I$.

\subsection{Claims and observable basis}
% Such a deduction should use, as much as possible, the observational basis, that is, the propositions of the form $α \ileadsto p$ that can be obtained experimentally.
Note first that given a relation ${\allleadstog} ∈ \allleadstos$, $\powerset{\allleadstog}$ denotes the set of relations over $(I × \allhists) × P$ that refine $\allleadstog$ (thus, each element $f ∈ \powerset{\allleadstog}$ is right-unique but non necessarily total, meaning that it does not necessarily cover $I × \allhists$).

Given a pair $(I, \allargs)$, a claim $C$ is a set of possibilities for $\allleadsto$, thus, $C ⊆ \allleadstos$.
Given a formula $ψ$, let the corresponding claim be $C_ψ = \set{{\allleadstog} ∈ \allleadstos \suchthat {\allleadstog} \text{ satisfies } ψ}$.
A claim is trivial iff it is the trivial claim, defined as $C = \allleadstos$.
A claim is finite iff its cardinal is finite (meaning that it is compatible with a finite number of possibilities).
Let $C^⊆ = ∪_{{\allleadstog} ∈ C} \powerset{\allleadstog} = \set{c ⊆ (I × \allhists) × P \suchthat ∃ {\allleadstog} ∈ C \suchthat c ∈ \powerset{\allleadstog}}$ denote the set of non necessarily complete possibilities compatible with $C$ (thus, each element $c ∈ C^⊆$ is right-unique but non necessarily total, meaning that it does not necessarily cover $(I × \allhists)$).
Note that $C^⊆$ is downwards closed, meaning that $∀ c ∈ C^⊆: c' ⊆ c ⇒ c' ∈ C^⊆$.

Define the increasing sets of paths as the set $\incr(\allhists) = \set{H \fsub \allhists \suchthat ∀\hist, \histp ∈ H: \card{\hist} ≤ \card{\histp} ⇒ \hist ⊆ \histp} ⊆ \powerset{\allhists}$. A set of paths $H ∈ \incr(\allhists)$ can be strictly ordered by the lengths of the paths in $H$ and can thus also be considered as a sequence of paths.
Note that $\incr(\allhists)$ includes both the empty set of paths $∅$ and the set of paths $\set{∅}$ containing only the empty path.
A non empty set of paths $H ∈ \incr(\allhists)$ has a unique maximal element $\set{\max H} = \set{\hist ∈ H \suchthat ∀ \histp ∈ H: \histp ⊆ \hist}$.
A non empty set of paths $H ∈ \incr(\allhists)$ contains all its prefixes (meaning, $∀ \hist ∈ H: \set{\histp ⊆ \hist} ⊆ H$) iff $H = \intvl{\max H}$.
Let $\finite(\incr(\allhists)^I)$ denote the functions $f: I ↦ \incr(\allhists)$ with finite support, thus, satisfying $\card{f^{-1}(\incr(\allhists) ∖ ∅)} ∈ ℕ$.

Thus, given ${\allleadstog} ∈ \allleadstos$, the set $Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstog}$ is the set of observations compatible with ${\allleadstog}$.
Similarly, an observation $θ$ is compatible with a claim $C$ iff
$θ ∈ ∪_{{\allleadstog} ∈ C} \powerset{\allleadstog}$, equivalently, iff
$θ ∈ C^⊆$.
The set $Θ ∩ C^⊆$ is the set of observations compatible with the claim.
This set is downwards-closed, as both $Θ$ and $C^⊆$ are.

A subset of triples $T ⊆ \allhists × I × P$ is unobservable iff it cannot be observed together, that is, iff $T ∉ Θ$. If $\card{T} ∉ ℕ$, $T$ is unobservable. A finite subset of triples $T$ is unobservable iff some subset $T' ⊆ T$ with $\card{T'} = 2$ is unobservable (unobservability comes from a given individual’s observations, for that one, the paths are not increasing, and as the subset relation is transitive, two elements suffice to determine this fact).

It is important to realize that $Θ$ is the set of \emph{a priori} possible observations, meaning, those that are conceivable, possibly counter-factually. The set of observations that can result from actually observing the behavior ${\allleadsto}$ is 
$Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadsto} = \set*{\restr{{\allleadsto}}{Q} \suchthat Q ∈ \allpriobss}$.
Such an observation $θ ∈ Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadsto}$ is called a feasible observation.

\subsection{Verifiability}
Given an exposure protocol $(I, \allargs, {\allleadsto})$, we say that a claim $C$ \emph{holds} iff ${\allleadsto} ∈ C$. We are interested in criteria to determine whether a claim holds.
Such a criterion must be sound, meaning that it must imply, when successful, that $C$ holds, and must be realizeable, meaning that if $C$ holds, the criterion must be satisfiable using feasible observations only (or something like that). Note that such criterion also offers a way to show that $C$ fails (does not hold), equivalently, that $\allleadstos ∖ C$ holds.

The claim $C$ is verifiable in the world $\allleadstogo$ iff $∃ θ ∈ Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstogo} \suchthat ∀ θ ⊆ {\allleadstog}: {\allleadstog} ∈ C$. 
Possible verifiability mandates that some (possibly counter-factual) observation proves the claim (makes sure that it holds), thus, $C$ is possibly verifiable iff it is verifiable in some world; formally, iff $∃ θ ∈ Θ \suchthat ∀ θ ⊆ {\allleadstog}: {\allleadstog} ∈ C$. 
Established verifiability mandates verifiability in all compatible worlds; formally, 
$∀\allleadstogo ∈ C: ∃ θ ∈ Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstogo} \suchthat ∀ θ ⊆ {\allleadstog'}: {\allleadstog'} ∈ C$. 
\begin{example}
  The claim $C$ being $[∀i: (a) \ileadsto φ] ∨ (b) \ileadsto φ$
  is possibly verifiable but fails established verifiability.
\end{example}
Verifiedness mandates verifiability in the actual world; formally, $∃ θ ∈ Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstoo} \suchthat ∀ θ ⊆ {\allleadstog}: {\allleadstog} ∈ C$.
Verifiedness
is sound (\cref{th:vImpliesHolds}), but even the weaker possible verifiability property is too demanding for many claims.
Indeed,
some sufficiently simple claims, such as the claim $C = \set{{\allleadstog} \suchthat (\ar) \ileadstog φ}$ that some given path leads some given individual to some given proposition, are possibly verifiable, but such claims are not strong enough to deduce much useful conclusion concerning anyone’s DJ.

A claim $C$ is \emph{informative} iff $π(C) = \set{π(\allleadstog) \suchthat {\allleadstog} ∈ C} ≠ (P ∪ \set{∅})^I$.
% A claim $C$ is \emph{informative} iff $∃ i \suchthat π(C)(i) ≠ P ∪ \set{∅}$, where $π(C) = \set{π(\allleadstog) \suchthat {\allleadstog} ∈ C}$.
A claim $C$ is \emph{informative beyond counting} iff $∃ i ∈ I \suchthat π_i(C) = \set{π_i(\allleadstog) \suchthat {\allleadstog} ∈ C} ≠ P ∪ \set{∅}$.

\begin{remark}
  Informativeness permits mere counting: the claim that exactly two persons deliberately believe $φ$ is informative. Also, the claim $C = \set{[∃ i \suchthat (a) \nileadstog φ] ∧ [∀i: (a) \nileadstog φ ⇒ φ' ∈ π_i(\allleadstog)]}$ is informative.
  Informativeness may be a desirable property (rather than informativeness beyond counting) as one may, for example, want to know whether a given proposition is believed by a majority of individuals.
  Note that 
  informativeness beyond counting implies informativeness.
\end{remark}

\Cref{th:infoNotVerif} shows that any informative claim is not possibly verifiable.
The fundamental reason is that any observation can be completed in a way that contradicts any conclusion that informativeness mandates (\cref{th:completable}).

That verifiability is not realizeable may seem disappointing, but is merely the adaptation to our context of the famous observation by Popper that proving general claims in science require more than verification.

To obtain realizeability in at least some interesting cases, we must relax soundness. We will be content that the criterion guarantees, when satisfied, that $C$ holds, when assuming some prior knowledge. We will look for the minimal required prior (for obtaining satisfiability when $C$ holds) and then will study (partial) grounding of this prior thanks to falsifiability.

Given $C, D ⊆ \allleadstos$, a claim $C$ is possibly verifiable under prior $D$ iff 
$∃ θ ∈ Θ ∩ D^⊆ \suchthat ∀ θ ⊆ {\allleadstog} ∈ D: {\allleadstog} ∈ C$.
Also, $C$ is verified under prior $D$ iff
$∃ θ ∈ Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadsto} ∩ D^⊆ \suchthat ∀ θ ⊆ {\allleadstog} ∈ D: {\allleadstog} ∈ C$; and is verified iff it is verified under the trivial prior.
Finally, $C$ satisfies established verifiability under prior $D$ iff
$∀\allleadstogo ∈ C ∩ D, ∃ θ ∈ Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstogo} \suchthat ∀ θ ⊆ {\allleadstogo'} ∈ D: {\allleadstogo'} ∈ C$.

If $D ⊆ D'$, then established verifiability under prior $D'$ implies established verifiability under prior $D$.

\Cref{th:vudImpliesHolds} proves that provided the prior is true, being verified under a prior suffices for holding.

We say that a prior $D$ proves a claim $C$ iff $D ⊆ C$. 

Provability by $D$ implies established verifiability under $D$ (just use verification $∅$).
For some priors, provability by $D$ is strictly stronger than established verifiability under $D$ (the claim $C$ being $(a) \ileadsto φ$ satisfies established verifiability under the trivial prior $D$ but is not provable by it). (TODO: for all priors?)

\Cref{th:toStricterClaims} shows that impossibilities propagate to stricter claims.
Together with \cref{th:minimalPriorForInformativeness}, it shows that 
the prior required must be at least as strong as claiming that if some observable holds, then some configuration of DJs is excluded.
\begin{remark}
  Suffices that the prior states something like: if almost all individuals deliberately believe $φ$, then everybody deliberately believe $φ$. This innocuous enough prior suffices because we request only informativeness, and indeed it only permits to restrict possibilities, not to determine anybody’s DJ.
\end{remark}

\Cref{th:noDecArgi,ex:noDecArg} illustrate about no decisive arguments.

\begin{remark}
  \label{th:noGeneral}
  No general prior is reasonable.
  A prior $D$ that states something general and reasonable such as “falsifiability implies correctness” and would be strong enough to allow determining that some substantial claim $C$ holds would mean that later rejecting $C$ would require rejecting the general prior. We want to keep the option of later rejecting $C$ (because one cannot be sure), and we want this to happen without having to reject a general claim such as the correctness of the method of falsifiability, rather just a particular claim about over-generalization.

  The prior must say that we can generalize this particular claim, which allow for later rejection.
  To prove that no general prior is reasonable, we might assume that two contradictory theories, $C$ ($(a)$ leads everyone to $φ$) and $C'$ ($(a)$ leads everyone to $φ$ except if $(a, b)$ does not), can be validated under the prior, considering some observations at some point. If under the prior, some obs proves C, then no further obs can prove C' under the same prior, so the prior must say something that is contradicted by the further obs, compatible with C', so the prior contradicts C'. So if the prior allows to validate C, then it contradicts C'. To be checked…
\end{remark}

\subsection{Which prior?}
Given $\allleadstogo$ and $C$, say that $C$ is
\begin{description}[font=\normalfont]
  \item[Persistently Observably False in $\allleadstogo$ ($\POF(\allleadstogo)$)] iff $∀θ ∈ Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstogo}, ∃θ ⊆ θ' ∈ Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstogo} \suchthat θ' ∉ C^⊆$;
  \item[Observably False (OF) in $\allleadstogo$ ($\OF(\allleadstogo)$)] iff $[∃θ ∈ Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstogo} \suchthat θ ∉ C^⊆]$, equivalently, $Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstogo} ⊈ C^⊆$;
  \item[False in $\allleadstogo$ ($\F(\allleadstogo)$)] iff $\allleadstogo ∉ C$.
\end{description}
We also say that $C$ is \emph{rejectable} iff $\OF(\allleadstoo)$.

Note that $∀C, \allleadstogo: \POF(\allleadstogo) ⇒ \OF(\allleadstogo) ⇒ \F(\allleadstogo)$ (\cref{th:ex-post} proves the second implication).
$C$ is said to suffer visibility issues in $\allleadstogo$ iff $¬\POF(\allleadstogo) ∧ \OF(\allleadstogo)$, which happens when some prefix hides the falsity of $C$ even though its falsity is observable when no prefix is involved.
$C$ suffers visibility issues iff $∃\allleadstogo \suchthat ¬\POF(\allleadstogo) ∧ \OF(\allleadstogo)$.
$C$ suffers no visibility issues in $\allleadstogo$ iff $\OF(\allleadstogo) ⇒ \POF(\allleadstogo)$, equivalently, iff $\OF(\allleadstogo) ⇔ \POF(\allleadstogo)$, and suffers no visibility issues iff $∀\allleadstogo: \OF(\allleadstogo) ⇒ \POF(\allleadstogo)$.

\begin{remark}
  \label{rk:dual}
  Verifiability and observable falsity have a dual character as
  $C$ satisfies established verifiability under prior $D$ iff 
  $∀\allleadstogo ∈ C ∩ D, \OF_{D ∖ C}(\allleadstogo)$ (\cref{th:dual}).
\end{remark}

We say that $C$ is \emph{falsifiable} iff $C ≠ {\allleadstos} ∧
\F(\allleadstogo) ⇒ \OF(\allleadstogo)$.

According to \cref{th:noGeneral}, the prior must depend on the claim.
One such is $C$-scrutiny, which mandates that $¬\OF(\allleadstoo) ∨ (¬\POF(\allleadstoo) ∧ \OF(\allleadstoo))$.
This is technically equivalent to mandating $¬\POF(\allleadstoo)$.

\begin{remark}
  Another way to describe $C$-scrutiny is that
  if the world is observably out of $C$, there is some prefix observation that hides this fact (formally: $\OF(\allleadstoo) ⇒ \OF(\allleadstoo) ∧ ¬\POF(\allleadstoo)$).
  
  Equivalently, 
  if $C$ is still observably falsifiable after any amount of observation, then no feasible observation would falsify it ($\POF(\allleadstoo) ⇒ ¬\OF(\allleadstoo)$).
  
  This corresponds to the prior $D 
  = \set{\allleadstoo \suchthat ∃θ ∈ Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstoo} \suchthat ∀θ ⊆ θ' ∈ Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstoo}: θ' ∈ C^⊆}
  $.

  Assuming, as the prior does, $¬\POF(\allleadstoo)$, leaves two possibilities: either there are visibility issues ($\OF(\allleadstoo) ∧ ¬\POF(\allleadstoo)$), or the claim is not observably false (thus is true, or false but inobservably so).

  When considering a claim $C$ with no visibility issues, this prior states $¬\OF(\allleadstoo)$.

  The reason for adopting the prior $¬\POF(\allleadstoo)$ instead of $¬\OF(\allleadstoo)$ is illustrated with
  $C_1$ is $(a) \ileadsto φ ∧ (b) \ileadsto φ$ for a given $i$: in some world (for example, where $(a) \ileadstog φ$ even though $(b) \nileadstog φ$), $¬\POF(\allleadstoo) ∧ \OF(\allleadstoo)$, thus we cannot assume that non-observation of falsification implies not OF, because it might also be due to observing that $(a) \ileadstog φ$.
  It also applies to $C_2 = \set{\allleadstoo \suchthat ∀i: (b) \ileadsto φ ∧ (a) \ileadsto[j] φ}$ for a given $j$, which in a world satisfying $(a) \nileadstog[j] φ$ and $∀i: (b) \ileadstog φ$ is $¬\POF(\allleadstogo)$ (consider the prefix $θ = \set{(b) \ileadstog[j] φ}$) and $\OF(\allleadstogo)$.
  (Note that if $C_2$ fails because, for some $i$, $(b) \nileadstog φ$,
  then $θ$ is not a hiding prefix observation and this is what we want.)

  With this example (considering again a world satisfying $(a) \nileadstog[j] φ$ and $∀i: (b) \ileadstog φ$), we see that formalizing $¬\POF$, independently of $\allleadstogo$,
  as $∃θ ∈ Θ \suchthat ∀θ ⊆ θ' ∈ Θ: θ' ∈ C^⊆$,
  fails, as this prevents the prefix $θ = \set{(b) \ileadstog[j] φ}$ from bringing not COF.
  The condition $∃θ ∈ Θ \suchthat ∀θ ⊆ θ' ∈ Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstog}: θ' ∈ C^⊆$ is also not suitable, as it is always satisfied: pick any $θ ∈ Θ$ such that $θ ∉ \powerset{\allleadstog}$.

  The following alternative prior, which might be considered for its apparent proximity with the chosen one, is also inadequate:
  $∀\allleadstogo, θ ∈ Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstogo}, [∃θ ⊆ θ' ∈ Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstogo} \suchthat θ' ∉ C^⊆] ⇒ [θ ∈ C^⊆]$.
  Consider again $C_1$ and $\allleadstogo$ such that $(a) \ileadstog φ ∧ (b) \nileadstog φ$.
  The chosen prior ($\POF(\allleadstogo) ⇒ ¬\OF(\allleadstogo)$) holds, as intuition commands, as the antecedent fails for the reason given above. The alternative prior however fails as $θ = \set{(b) \ileadstog φ}$ is completable only out of $C$.
  
  Alternatively, we could apply the prior only for the claims which have no visibility issues, by stating the following.
  $[∀\allleadstogo, ¬[∃θ ∈ Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstogo} \suchthat ∀θ ⊆ θ' ∈ Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstogo}: θ' ∈ C^⊆]] ⇒ [∀\allleadstogo, ∀θ ∈ Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstogo}: θ ∈ C^⊆]$.
  Consider $C_1$ is $(a) \ileadsto φ ∧ (b) \ileadsto φ$ for a given $i$ and a world st $(a) \ileadsto^1 φ ∧ (b) \nileadsto^1 φ$. This world has visibility issues. Consider a world st $(a) \ileadsto^2 φ ∧ (b) \ileadsto^2 φ$. This world has no visibility issues.
  The alternative prior does not say anything about $C$ (all worlds are possible).
  The original prior excludes the world 2.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Falsifiability}
We neglect temporarily the unconvincingness that arises when not COF and OF.
Thus, this section studies the prior $¬\OF(\allleadstoo)$. 
We consider it suitable even for claims satisfying not COF and OF for some worlds.

Any claim $C$ satisfies exactly one of these:
\begin{description}
  \item[falsifiable] iff $∀\allleadstogo ∉ C: \OF(\allleadstogo)$ and $∃\allleadstogo ∉ C$;
  \item[never falsifiable] iff $∀\allleadstogo ∉ C: ¬\OF(\allleadstogo)$, 
equivalently, iff $∀\allleadstogo: ¬\OF(\allleadstogo)$;
  \item[with mixed falsifiability] iff $∃\allleadstogo ∉ C \suchthat ¬\OF(\allleadstogo)$ and $∃\allleadstogo ∉ C \suchthat \OF(\allleadstogo)$.
\end{description}
Indeed, first implies $∃\allleadstogo ∉ C \suchthat \OF(\allleadstogo)$ which negates second, and the negation of the conjunction of the first two is $(∃\allleadstogo ∉ C \suchthat ¬\OF(\allleadstogo) ∨ ∄\allleadstogo ∉ C) ∧ (∃\allleadstogo ∉ C \suchthat \OF(\allleadstogo))$, equivalent to the third.

We want to study the following dichotomy.
Any claim $C$ satisfies exactly one of these:
\begin{description}
  \item[falsifiable or trivial] iff $∀\allleadstogo ∉ C: \OF(\allleadstogo)$, 
equivalently, iff $∀\allleadstogo: \F(\allleadstogo) ⇒ \OF(\allleadstogo)$,
still equivalently, iff $∀\allleadstogo: \F(\allleadstogo) ⇔ \OF(\allleadstogo)$;
  \item[not falsifiable and not trivial] iff $∃\allleadstogo ∉ C \suchthat ¬\OF(\allleadstogo)$, that is, whenever the implication $\F(\allleadstogo) ⇒ \OF(\allleadstogo)$ fails.
\end{description}

Distinguishing two cases among the latter situation leads to the following partition.
Any claim $C$ satisfies exactly one of these:
\begin{description}
  \item[falsifiable or trivial] iff $∀\allleadstogo ∉ C: \OF(\allleadstogo)$, 
    equivalently, iff $∀\allleadstogo: \F(\allleadstogo) ⇒ \OF(\allleadstogo)$,
    still equivalently, iff $∀\allleadstogo: \F(\allleadstogo) ⇔ \OF(\allleadstogo)$;
  \item[never falsifiable and not trivial] iff $∀\allleadstogo ∉ C: ¬\OF(\allleadstogo)$ and $∃\allleadstogo ∉ C$;
  \item[with mixed falsifiability] iff $∃\allleadstogo ∉ C \suchthat ¬\OF(\allleadstogo)$ and $∃\allleadstogo ∉ C \suchthat \OF(\allleadstogo)$.
\end{description}
Indeed, second implies $∃\allleadstogo ∉ C \suchthat ¬\OF(\allleadstogo)$ which negates first, and the negation of the conjunction of the first two is $∃\allleadstogo ∉ C \suchthat ¬\OF(\allleadstogo) ∧ (∃\allleadstogo ∉ C \suchthat \OF(\allleadstogo) ∨ ∄\allleadstogo ∉ C)$, equivalent to the third.

Here are typical examples.
\begin{description}
  \item[Falsifiable] $(a) \ileadsto φ$ (satisfies established verifiability); 
    $∀i: (a) \ileadsto φ$ (fails possible verifiability); $∀i: \ar \ileadstostg φ$ (fails possible verifiability) (\cref{ex:allParticular}).
  \item[Never falsifiable] $[∃i \suchthat (a) \ileadsto φ]$ which satisfies established verifiability; $[∀i: [(\ar) \ileadsto φ ∨ (\ar') \ileadsto φ]]$ (\cref{ex:disjR}) which fails possible verifiability; $[(\ar) \ileadsto φ ∨ (\ar') \ileadsto φ]$ which satisfies established verifiability; $[∀ i: (\ar) \ileadstog φ] ∨ [∀ i: (\ar, \ar) \ileadstog φ] ∨ …$ (\cref{ex:bigor}) which fails possible verifiability.
  \item[With mixed falsifiability] $[∃i \suchthat (a) \ileadsto φ] ∧ (b) \ileadsto φ$ (proof:  $∀\allleadstogo ∉ C: \OF(\allleadstogo) ⇔ (b) \nileadstog φ$) (satisfies established verifiability);
    $[∃i \suchthat (a) \ileadsto φ] ∧ [∀i: (b) \ileadsto φ]$ (proof: $∀\allleadstogo ∉ C: \OF(\allleadstogo) ⇔ [∃i \suchthat (b) \nileadstog φ]$) (fails possible verifiability);
    “Exactly two of the three paths in $\set{∅, (\ar), (\ar')}$ lead $i$ to $φ$ and the remaining one leads $i$ to $φ'$” (\cref{ex:threeFs}) which fails possible verifiability.
\end{description}

It is important to note that falsifiability does not depend on $\allleadsto$: whether falsifiability holds can be determined analytically, meaning, by considering only the content of the claim, without considering the behavior of individuals.

\Cref{th:holdsiff} shows that falsifiability indeed fills the mentioned gap:
falsifiability guarantees that a non trivial claim holds if, and only if, it is not rejectable.

\begin{remark}
  Making a claim more precise (meaning going from a claim $C$ to $C' \ssub C$) can make it falsifiable, or conversely, can make it unfalsifiable.
  Given $\hist, i$, consider the claim $C_2 = \hist \ileadstog φ$. It is falsifiable.
  Now consider the claim $C = C_1 ∩ C_2$ with $C_1$ being the claim “every individual chooses $φ$ after seeing solely either $\ar$ or $\ar'$”. It is more precise but unfalsifiable.
  But we can make it falsifiable again by making it even more precise (picking any single relation).
  (See \cref{rq:precise}.)
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}
  Given any claim $C$,
  the prior $¬\OF(\allleadstoo)$ proves $C$ iff 
  $C$ is falsifiable or trivial.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  $D$ proves $C$ means, by definition, $∀\allleadstogo: ¬\OF(\allleadstogo) ⇒ ¬\F(\allleadstogo)$.
\end{proof}

Provability by $¬\OF(\allleadstoo)$ (equivalently, falsifiability or triviality) is strictly stronger than established verifiability under $¬\OF(\allleadstoo)$; and established verifiability is strictly stronger than established verifiability under $¬\OF(\allleadstoo)$. (TODO: with any $D ⊆ D'$, EV under $D'$ is strictly stronger than EV under $D$?)

\begin{example}
  The claim $C_1 = \set{∃i \suchthat (a) \ileadsto φ}$, with infinitely many individuals, satisfies established verifiability (consider $θ = \set{(a) \ileadsto φ}$), hence, established verifiability under $¬\OF(\allleadstoo)$, but is not provable by $¬\OF(\allleadstoo)$.
  Similarly for $C_2 = \set{(a) \ileadsto φ ∨ (b) \ileadsto φ}$ and $θ = \set{(a) \ileadsto φ}$.
  
  Also, consider $C_3$ being $∀j: (c) \ileadsto[j] φ$, $C_4$ being $(d) \ileadsto[j] φ ∧ (e) \ileadsto[j] φ$ and $C$ being $C_1$ or $C_2$ together with $C_3$ or $C_4$ (assuming infinitely many individuals if using $C_1$ or $C_3$).
  Then, $C$ is fails established verifiability (due to $C_3$ or $C_4$) and $¬\OF(\allleadstoo)$ does not prove $C$ (due to $C_1$ or $C_2$), but $C$ satisfies established verifiability under prior $¬\OF(\allleadstoo)$ (with verification $(a) \ileadsto φ$).
\end{example}
  
Note that if a claim is not provable by $¬\OF(\allleadstoo)$ (equivalently, some world is out of $C$ and NOT OF), then some world is out of $C$ and NOT COF.

It is probably useful to remember that $¬\OF(\allleadstogo)$ states that 
negating $C$, in that world, requires incompatible, or infinitely many, triples: 
the triples completable only out of C are all unobservable. 
Indeed, $Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstogo} ⊆ C^⊆$ is equivalent to $∀T ∈ \powerset{\allleadstogo}, T ∉ C^⊆ ⇒ T ∉ Θ$.
When a claim is never falsifiable, thus $∀\allleadstogo, ¬\OF(\allleadstogo)$, we obtain $Θ ⊆ C^⊆$.

\subsection{Observational exclusion and completion}
$S ⊆ \allleadstos$ is observationally excludable iff 
$∃ θ ∈ Θ \suchthat θ ∉ S^⊆$,
equivalently, iff $Θ ⊈ S^⊆$,
equivalently, iff $∃\allleadstogo ∉ S \suchthat \OF(\allleadstogo)$,
equivalently, iff $∃\allleadstogo \suchthat \OF(\allleadstogo)$,
equivalently, iff $S$ is NOT never falsifiable.

$D$ is observationally completable to $C$ iff 
$∃ θ ∈ (Θ ∩ D^⊆) \suchthat θ ∉ (D ∖ C)^⊆$.
\Cref{th:obsComp} shows the equivalence with possible verifiability under prior $D$.

Converting a claim to disjunctive normal form is useful to characterize its compatible observations, as \cref{th:dnf} indicates.
\begin{example}
  Consider six different arguments $a_1, a'_1, a_2, a'_2, a_3, a'_3 ∈ \allargs$
  and the following claim $E$:

  \subsection{Argumentative theories of deliberated choice}
  \label{sec:dynamic}
  An argumentative theory of deliberated choice $γ$ is defined as a tuple $(\pga, \dg)$, where $\pga ∈ P$ is the proposition supported by the theory and $\dg$ is a defense relation.

  The defense relation $\dg ⊆ \allhists × \allargs$ defines the argumentation strategy of the theory. Informally speaking, it defines the arguments that the theory claims will convince the individuals in $I$ to choose $\pga$. Assuming that some path $\hist$ challenges the theory’s intended conclusion in favor of $\pga$ (as judged by the individual), $\dg(\hist)$ is the argument that the theory plays as a counter-argument to the path $\hist$.

  Formally speaking, a defense relation $\dg ⊆ \allhists × \allargs$ is a right-unique relation satisfying bounded depth (to be defined shortly). Right-unique means that given any $\hist ∈ \allhists$, $\dg(\hist) = \set{\ar ∈ \allargs \suchthat (\hist, \ar) ∈ \dg}$ is empty or is a singleton.
  As is usual for right-unique relations, we write $\dg(\hist) = \ar$ instead of $\dg(\hist) = \set{\ar}$ when $\dg(\hist)$ is a singleton (and $\dg(\hist) = ∅$ otherwise).
  Given $H ⊆ \allhists$, define $\dg(H) = \set{\dg(\hist) \suchthat α ∈ H} ⊆ \allargs$.
  Given $\ar_0, \ar_1 ∈ \allargs$, say that $\ar_1$ defends $\ar_0$
  iff $∃ \hist ∈ \allhists \suchthat \ar_0 ∈ R(\hist) ∧ \dg(\hist) = \ar_1$, equivalently,
  iff $\ar_0 ∈ R(\dg^{-1}(\ar_1))$, equivalently,
  iff $\ar_1 ∈ \dg(\set{\hist \suchthat \ar_0 ∈ R(\hist)})$.

  Say that $γ$ has depth at least $k ∈ \Np$ iff $∃ (α_i, 1 ≤ i ≤ k) \suchthat ∀ 1 ≤ i ≤ k, \dg(\set{α_j \suchthat 1 ≤ j ≤ i - 1}) ⊊ \dg(\set{α_j \suchthat 1 ≤ j ≤ i}) ∧ ∀ 1 ≤ i ≤ k - 1, α_i ∪ \dg(α_i) ⊆ α_{i + 1}$.
  The bounded depth condition on the defense relation imposes that for some $k ∈ \Np$, $γ$ does not have depth at least $k$.
  The depth of a theory is defined as the smallest value $k ∈ ℕ$ such that it does not have depth at least $k + 1$.
  For example, $γ$ has depth $0$ iff $\dg = ∅$; and $γ$ has depth $1$ iff $[∃ α_1 \suchthat \dg(α_1) ≠ ∅] ∧ [∀ α_1, α_2 \suchthat \dg(α_1) ≠ ∅ ∧ α_1 ∪ \dg(α_1) ⊆ α_2: \dg(α_2) ⊆ \dg(α_1)]$.

  \commentOC{Intuition. Invoke $\dg$ at $α$ only when $α$ fails. Permit to invoke a previous defense again as maybe the counter was too long to perform when summarized.   More precisely, given $i ∈ I$, it claims that $\arga$ either is a decisive argument for $\pga$, or, if $\arga$ is not, that the theory will defend the argument by replying to every counter-argument to $\arga$ with an argument that either is a decisive argument for $\pga$ or, in turn, will be defended by the theory, and so on as long as the theory has new counter-arguments to produce; the theory claiming that some argument along that chain is decisive.

  It is said to be truthful iff this claim is correct.

  \begin{axiom}[Answerability]
    \label{ax:ans}
    $∀ i ∈ I, \hist ∈ \allhists, \ar ∈ \gargsi$:
    \begin{equation}
      \left.\begin{aligned}
         & \hist \nileadsto \pga                                        \\
         & (\hist, \ar) \ileadsto \pga                                  \\
         & ∀ \ar' ∈ \allargs: (\hist, \ar, \ar') \ileadsto \pga
      \end{aligned}\right\}
      ⇒ \ar \ileadstost \pga.
    \end{equation}
  \end{axiom}
  \footnote{$a_1, a_2, a_3$ NOT and $a_1, a_2, a_3, a_4$ YES and $a_1', a_2, a_3, a_4$ NO imply that $a_1, a_2, a_3, a_4, a'_1$ NO.
    Generalizing: $α$ NO and $α, a_4$ YES and $α', a_4$ NO imply that $∃ a' ∈ α' \suchthat α, a_4, a'$ NO.
    Let’s try with this too powerful axiom, but then we should weaken it, as it could be that $a_1$ AND $a_4$ TOGETHER are decisive, whence the axiom fails. We should have then some argument $a_1 + a_4$ to replace them, and…?
    In summary, we want to avoid that $a$ is not decisive but we can’t observe it when starting with some $α$ even though $α$ has also no decisive arguments: if $α$ has no decisive argument, then it must be possible to counter it.

    In fact, we might need (a weakening of) the following. If $a_1$ is “decisive” when starting with some $α$ (meaning $(α, a_1, a')$ YES for all arguments $a'$), then $a_1$ is decisive. Hopefully: if $(α, a')$ YES for all arguments $a'$, then some $a ∈ α$ is decisive.}
  \footnote{
    There is a problem. Consider the following may hold for some $i$. $a_1, a_5, a_2$ not convincing, $a_1, a_4$ not convincing, whil $a_1, a_5$ and $a_1, a_4, a_2$ and other variants convincing. Then for all $i$ one of $a_1$ or $a_2$ will appear to be decisive although for those people neither is.

    Problem might be solved by observing that $(a_1, a_5, a_2, a_1)$ convincing means that $a_1$ is decisive after all; and not convincing means that we have observed not truthfulness.

    We want that if the theory is invalid (thus some $i$ is not convinced), then either axiom fails or the theory is experimentally falsifiable.
    In the following example, the theory seems invalid but the axiom holds and the theory can’t seem to be defeated experimentally.

    Imagine $i$ thinks as follows. $a_1$ counter $a_5$; $a_2$ counters $a_4$; but $a_4$ and $a_5$ together make $a_1$ and $a_2$ invalid, so that $(a_4, a_5)$ counter both $a_1$ and $a_2$ (and also $a_1$ and $a_2$ together). BUT $a_5, a_4, a_1$ should then not convince.

    The solution is: after $a1, a5, a2$, $a1$ appears decisive, thus also $a1,a5, a2, a1, a4$ ok. Then some subset $a$ of $a1 ∪ a_5 ∪ a2$ should satisfy $a, a4$ OK and replace $a1$. And $a$ is decisive.

    $∀ i ∈ I, \hist^j ∈ (\allhists)^n$:
    \begin{equation}
      [∀ j: \hist^j \ileadstost \pga] ⇒ [∃ \ar ∈ \allargs \suchthat ∀ j: (\hist^j, \ar) \ileadstost \pga].

  \begin{axiom}[Reinstatement]
    \label{ax:r}
    $∀ i ∈ I, \hist ∈ \allhists, \ar_1, \ar_2 ∈ \gargsi, \ar ∈ \allargs$:
    \begin{equation}
      \left.\begin{aligned}
         & (\hist, \ar_1, \ar) \nileadsto \pga            \\
         & (\hist, \ar_1, \ar, \ar_2) \ileadsto \pga      \\
         & (\hist, \ar_1, \ar, \ar_2, \ar) \ileadsto \pga
      \end{aligned}\right\}
      ⇒
      \left\{\begin{aligned}
         & (\hist, \ar_2) \ileadsto \pga                                                      \\
         & (\hist, \ar_2, \ar) \ileadsto \pga                                                 \\
         & ∀ \ar': (\hist, a_2, a') \nileadsto \pga ⇒ (\hist, a_1, a') \nileadsto \pga.
      \end{aligned}\right.
    \end{equation}
  \end{axiom}
  \begin{example}[Reinstatement is required]
    Consider $a_1$ not decisive because of $a$ and then $(a_1, a, a_2) \ileadsto p$. But still $a_2$ not decisive: $a2 \nileadsto p$.
  \end{example}

  A path can be shortened to its first $k ∈ \intvl{0, \card{\hist}}$ elements, which is denoted by $\restr{\hist}{\intvl{1, k}}$, and “shortening” a path beyond its length yields the same path again, thus given $k ∈ ℤ$, $\restr{\hist}{\intvl{1, k}} = \restr{\hist}{\intvl{1, \min\set{\card{\hist}, k}}}$.
  Define a path as being decisive for $p$, $\hist \ileadstost p$, iff $\hist \ileadsto p ∧ ∀ \ar ∈ \allargs: (\hist, \ar) \ileadsto p$, or equivalently, iff $∀ \hist' \suchthat \card{\hist} ≤ \card{\hist'} ≤ \card{\hist} + 1 ∧ \restr{\hist'}{\intvl{1, \card{\hist}}} = \hist: \hist' \ileadsto p$. Thus, $\hist \nileadstost p ⇔ ∃ \ar ∈ \allargs \suchthat (\hist, \ar) \nileadsto p$ (or…?).

  \begin{axiom}[Self-sufficiency of positive arguments]
    $∀ \ar_1, \ar_2 ∈ \gargsi, \ar ∈ \allargs, \hist ∈ \allhists$:
    \begin{equation}
      \left.\begin{aligned}
         & (\hist, \ar_1) \nileadstost \pga \\
         & (\hist, \ar_2) \nileadstost \pga
      \end{aligned}\right\}
      ⇒ (\hist, \ar_1, \ar_2) \nileadstost \pga.
    \end{equation}
  \end{axiom}

  \begin{theorem}[Validity]
    If an argumentative theory $(\pga, \arga, \dg)$ is truthful and satisfy \cref{ax:ans,ax:r} and $\allargs$ is finite, then the theory is correct.
  \end{theorem}
  \begin{proof}
    Consider any $i ∈ I$.
    We have to show that $\pga ∈ Φ_i$.
    Assume depth $3$.
    Assume that $\arga$ and $\ar_2$ are not decisive. Let’s show that $\ar_3$ is decisive.
    Assume that $(a_4, \arga, \ar_2, a) \nileadsto \pga$.
    By TF, $(a_4, \arga, \ar_2, a, \ar_3) \ileadstost \pga$.
    By Reinst, $(a_4, \arga, \ar_2, \ar_3) \ileadstost \pga$.
    By Self-s, $(a_4, \arga, \ar_3) \ileadstost \pga$.
    By Self-s, $(a_4, \ar_3) \ileadstost \pga$.

    Now consider $(\ar_5, \ar_2) \ileadsto \pga$. (Or any such path not ending with $\ar_3$.)
    We want $(\ar_5, \ar_2, \ar_3) \ileadstost \pga$.
    By Answ, $∃ a \suchthat (\ar_5, \ar_2, a) \nileadsto \pga$.
  \end{proof}

  This definition of truthfulness is adopted because of our goal of distinguishing empirical claims. Truthfulness, so defined, can be falsified empirically: if it does not hold, it is possible to prove this empirically by exhibiting a counter-example in the form of a tuple $i, k, (a_j)_{j ∈ \intvl{1, k}}, α$ such that … (to be completed).

  Note that a static theory $(p, \ar)$ can be considered as an argumentative theory $(p, \ar, ∅)$, and $(p, \ar)$ is truthful in the sense of \cref{sec:static} iff $(p, \ar, ∅)$ is truthful in the sense of \cref{def:truth}.

  Associated with an argumentative theory also comes the following axiom, that mandates a more general version of the following requirement: if some argument $\ar_2$ defends some argument $\ar_1$, and if $\ar_2$ leads $i$ to opt for $\pga$ whenever it immediately follows $\ar_1$, then omitting $\ar_1$ would still have lead $i$ to opt for $\pga$.

  \begin{axiom}[Omitting defended arguments]
    \label{ax:omitting}
    If the theory is truthful, then $∀ i ∈ I, k ∈ \Np, l ∈ \intvl{2, k}, (\ar_j)_{j ∈ \intvl{1, k}} ∈ \allargs^k, \hist, β ∈ \allhists \suchthat [\ar_k ∈ \histend ∧ ∀ j ∈ \intvl{2, k}: \ar_j ∈ \dgi(\ar_{j - 1})]:
      (β, \ar_{l - 1}, \ar_l, \hist) \ileadsto \pga ⇒ (β, \ar_l, \hist) \ileadsto \pga$.
  \end{axiom}
  This is called an axiom because it cannot be falsified using observables. It has to be accepted on the basis of the rest of the observations and on the contents of the defending arguments. As \cref{ex:lichtenstein} suggests, theories can be built so as to make this axiom plausible by embedding within defending arguments the content of the defended argument.

  An argumentative theory is said to be correct iff $∀ i ∈ I: \pga ∈ \iprops$.
  As previously, it is said to be valid iff its truthfulness leads to its correctness, except that the above axiom needs now be included as a price for the added flexibility of the theory.
  \begin{theorem}[Validity]
    If an argumentative theory is truthful, then \cref{ax:omitting} implies that the theory is correct.
  \end{theorem}
  \begin{proof}[Sketch of proof]
    Assume, to start with, that the theory is of depth 2, more precisely, $\arga$ is not decisive because $\hist \nileadsto \pga, \arga ∈ \histend$ and $\dgi(\arga) = \ar_2$ with no further hist against it.

    Consider any $i ∈ I$.
    Assume $\arga$ is not decisive.
    Consider any $\hist \suchthat \ar_2 ∈ \histend$. We want to show that $\hist \ileadsto \pga$.

    IF $\hist$ contains $\arga$ before $\ar_2$, done by TF.
    Otherwise, define $hist'$ which has a1 just before a2.
    Apply TF on hist' then apply axiom.

    Consider now a theory of depth 3.
    Consider any $i ∈ I$.
    Assume $\arga$ not decisive, $\ar_2$ not decisive.
    Consider any $\hist \suchthat \ar_3 ∈ \histend$. We want to show that $\hist \ileadsto \pga$.

    If it includes a1 then a2, ok thanks to truthfulness.
    Otherwise, build hist3 that has a1 then a2 just before a3.
    That’s ok thanks to TF.
    By axiom, hist2 with only a2 just before a3 is thus ok.
    By axiom, hist is thus ok.
  \end{proof}

\begin{theorem}
  \label{th:vudImpliesHolds}
  Given a claim $D$ such that ${\allleadsto} ∈ D$, if a claim $C$ is verified under prior $D$, then $C$ holds.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  Consider any $θ$ that satisfies verifiedness under $D$. 
  We have $θ ⊆ {\allleadsto}$.
  By hypothesis, ${\allleadsto} ∈ D$.
  Therefore, by verifiedness, ${\allleadsto} ∈ C$.
\end{proof}
Using the trivial prior $D = \allleadstos$, we obtain this corollary.
\begin{corollary}
  \label{th:vImpliesHolds}
  If a claim is verified, it holds.
\end{corollary}

{
  \crefname{enumi}{property}{properties}
  \Crefname{enumi}{Property}{Properties}
  \begin{theorem}
    \label{th:vud}
    For any $θ ∈ Θ$ and claims $C, D$, 
    these properties are equivalent:
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item \label{it:unverifD} $∀ θ ⊆ {\allleadstog} ∈ D: {\allleadstog} ∈ C$,
      \item \label{it:nocomplD} $D ∩ \set{{\allleadstog} \suchthat θ ⊆ {\allleadstog}} ⊆ C$,
      \item \label{it:notcomplE} $θ ∉ (D ∖ C)^⊆$,
      \item \label{it:obscovers} there exists a finite disjoint partition $\set{S_k}$ of $D ∖ C$ such that $∀k, ∃t ∈ θ \suchthat \set{t} ∉ S_k^⊆$ (equivalently, $t ∉ ⋃S_k$).
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{theorem}
  \begin{proof}
    \Cref{it:unverifD,it:nocomplD} assert that 
    $∀ θ ⊆ {\allleadstog}: {\allleadstog} ∈ D ⇒ {\allleadstog} ∈ C$, 
    % equivalently, 
    % $∀ θ ⊆ {\allleadstog}: {\allleadstog} ∉ D ∨ {\allleadstog} ∈ C$,
    equivalently, 
    $∄ θ ⊆ {\allleadstog} \suchthat {\allleadstog} ∈ D ∧ {\allleadstog} ∉ C$, which is \cref{it:notcomplE}.

    To establish that \cref{it:notcomplE} implies \cref{it:obscovers}, consider any claim $E$ and $θ ∉ E^⊆$.
    Given any $t ∈ θ$, define $S_t = \set{{\allleadstog} \suchthat t ∉ {\allleadstog}} ∩ E$.
    Define $S = ⋃_{t ∈ θ} S_t$.

    Consider any ${\allleadstog} ∈ E$. As $θ ⊈ {\allleadstog}$, $∃ t ∈ θ \suchthat t ∉ {\allleadstog}$, thus, ${\allleadstog} ∈ S$.
    It follows that $S = E$.

    As $θ$ is finite, we can finitely enumerate $t ∈ θ$, and define $S_1 = S_{t_1}$ and for any $2 ≤ k ≤ \card{θ}$, $S_k = S_{t_k} ∖ ⋃_{1 ≤ k' < k} S_{t_{k'}}$. We obtain $E = S = ⋃_{1 ≤ k ≤ \card{θ}} S_k$, thus it is a disjoint partition of $E$, and as $\set{t_k} ∉ S_{t_k}^⊆$ by construction of $S_{t_k}$, also $\set{t_k} ∉ S_k^⊆$.

    Finally, negating \cref{it:notcomplE}, if $θ$ is completable to some set covered by $\set{S_k}$, then $θ$ is completable to some set $S_k$, which contradicts \cref{it:obscovers}.
  \end{proof}

\begin{theorem}
  \label{th:dnf}
  Given any $E$
  and a cover $\mathcal{S}$ of $E$ (a set of non-empty sets whose union contains $E$) 
  such that $∀ S ∈ \mathcal{S}, ∃ f_S ⊆ (\allhists × I) × P \suchthat S = \mathop{↑} f_S$ with $f_S$ right unique,
  given any $f ⊆ (\allhists × I) × P$: 
  $f ∉ E^⊆$ 
  is equivalent to 
  $∀ S ∈ \mathcal{S}, ∃ t ∈ f \suchthat \set{t} ∉ S^⊆$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  Pick any $S ∈ \mathcal{S}$ and $f_S \suchthat S = \mathop{↑} f_S$.
  Given any $p ∈ f_S$, define $S_p = \mathop{↑} \set{p}$, obtaining $S = ⋂_{p ∈ f_S} S_p$.
  Thus $f ∪ f_S$ is not completable to a function (if $f ∪ f_S$ is completable to a function, that function is in $S$, thus in $E$, thus $f$ compatible with $E$).
  It follows that $f ∪ f_S$ is not right unique,
  thus, that some $t ∈ f$ contradicts $f_S$.
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}
  $(a ≠ b) ∨ (a ∧ c)$ is equivalent to $(a ≠ b) ∨ (b ∧ c)$, thus there is no unique minimal DNF.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}[Disjunct with incompatible paths, not rejectable]
  \label{ex:disjR}
  Given $\ar ≠ \ar' ∈ \allargs$,
  the claim $∀ i: [(\ar) \ileadstog φ ∨ (\ar') \ileadstog φ]$ is not rejectable.
  In fact,
  $Θ ⊆ C^⊆$.
  Consider any $θ ∈ Θ$ and let us prove that $θ ∈ ∪_{{\allleadstog} ∈ C} \powerset{\allleadstog}$.
  By definition of $Θ$, $θ^{-1}(P)$ may not contain both $((\ar), i)$ and $((\ar'), i)$, thus letting $t$ denote one of these pairs not in $θ^{-1}(P)$ and defining $r = (t, φ)$, pick any ${\allleadstog'}$ such that $θ ⊆ {\allleadstog'} ∧ t ∈ {\allleadstog'}$. We have $θ ∈ \powerset{\allleadstog'}$ and ${\allleadstog'} ∈ C$.
\end{example}
\Cref{ex:disjR} shows a claim that is not rejectable, irrespective of the exposure protocol.
In particular, the example applies when $C$ does not hold (that is, when ${\allleadsto} ∉ C$). We conclude that a claim may fail to hold but still be not rejectable.
It follows that non-rejection cannot be a sufficient condition for validating a claim.

\begin{remark}
  \Cref{ex:bigor,ex:threeFs} present situations where, similarly, the claim may fail to hold but still be not rejectable.
  In \cref{ex:bigor}, non rejection is due to finiteness of observations.
  In \cref{ex:threeFs}, rejection depends on ${\allleadsto}$.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}[Big disjunct, non rejectable]

\begin{example}[Big disjunct unfals iff infinite]
  \label{ex:existsParticular}
  Given any $\ar ∈ \allargs$ and an infinite $I$, the claim $∃ i \suchthat \ar \ileadstostg φ$ is strongly unfalsifiable: any $θ ∈ Θ$ only has finitely many $i$ in its triples, thus, some $i$ is not reached by $θ$, thus $θ ∈ C^⊆$.

\end{example}

\begin{example}[Three functions, may be rejectable]
  \label{ex:threeFs}
  Consider $i ∈ I$, $ar ≠ ar' ∈ \allargs$ and the three paths $\hists = \set{∅, (\ar), (\ar')}$.
  The claim “Exactly two of the three paths in $\hists$ lead $i$ to $φ$ and the remaining one leads $i$ to $φ'$” is not rejectable iff ${\ileadsto}$ associates, in order, $(∅, (\ar), (\ar'))$ to either
  $(φ', φ, φ)$,
  $(φ, φ', φ)$,
  $(φ, φ, φ')$,
  $(φ, φ, φ)$ or
  $(φ, φ', φ')$.

  Indeed, if ${\ileadsto}$ satisfies this, then either it holds and by \cref{th:ex-post} it is not rejectable, or it associates this triplet to $(φ, φ, φ)$ or
  $(φ, φ', φ')$ in which case, as $(\ar)$ and $(\ar')$ cannot be together in $θ$, any $θ ∈ Θ$ with $θ ⊆ {\allleadsto}$ satisfy $θ ∈ C^⊆$.

\end{example}

\begin{theorem}
  $D ∖ C$ is falsifiable iff ($C$ satisfies established verifiability under prior $D$ AND
  $∀\allleadstogo ∉ D: \OF_{D ∖ C}(\allleadstogo)$).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  \Cref{th:dual} shows that
  $C$ satisfies established verifiability under prior $D$
  iff $∀\allleadstogo ∉ \overline{C ∩ D}: \OF_{D ∖ C}(\allleadstogo)$.
  Note that
  $D ∖ C$ is falsifiable iff 
  $∀\allleadstogo ∉ (\overline{C ∩ D} ∩ D): \OF_{D ∖ C}(\allleadstogo)$
  (because $D ∖ C = \overline{C ∩ D} ∩ D$).
  This implies established verifiability under prior $D$.
  The converse fails (thus $∀\allleadstogo ∉ \overline{C ∩ D}: \OF_{D ∖ C}(\allleadstogo)$ but $¬[∀\allleadstogo ∉ (\overline{C ∩ D} ∩ D): \OF_{D ∖ C}(\allleadstogo)]$)
  exactly when 
  $∃\allleadstogo ∉ D \suchthat ¬\OF_{D ∖ C}(\allleadstogo)$.

  Write (1) for the LHS.
  Write (2) for EV.
  Write (3) for $∃\allleadstogo ∉ D \suchthat ¬\OF_{D ∖ C}(\allleadstogo)$.

  We know that $(1) ⇒ (2)$.

  We know that $((2) \not⇒ (1)) ⇔ (3)$,
  in other words, $(2) ∧ ¬(1) ⇔ (3)$,
  from which follows, using the forward direction, $(2) ∧ ¬(3) ⇒ (1)$,
  and using the negation, $(1) ⇒ ¬(3)$, which together with $(1) ⇒ (2)$ yields $(1) ⇒ (2) ∧ ¬(3)$.
\end{proof}
\begin{corollary}
  $\allleadstos ∖ C$ is falsifiable iff $C$ satisfies established verifiability.
\end{corollary}
\begin{theorem}
  For any $θ ∈ Θ$, the following statements are equivalent.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $θ ∉ C^⊆$,
    \item $∀θ ⊆ \allleadstogo, \allleadstogo ∉ C$,
    \item $∀θ ⊆ \allleadstogo: \OF(\allleadstogo)$,
    \item $θ ∉ (¬\OF_C(\allleadstoo))^⊆$.
  \end{itemize}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  1 and 2 are equivalent by definition.
  From 1 to 3, that’s because for any such $\allleadstogo$, we have indeed $θ ∈ Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstogo} \suchthat θ ∉ C^⊆$.
\end{proof}
\begin{theorem}
  For any claim $C$, with $D$ being $¬\OF_C(\allleadstoo)$,
  $∀\allleadstogo: \OF_C(\allleadstogo) ⇔ \OF_D(\allleadstogo) ⇒ \OF_{D ∖ C}(\allleadstogo)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  $\OF_C(\allleadstogo) ⇔ ∃θ ∈ Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstogo} \suchthat θ ∉ C^⊆ ⇔ ∃θ ∈ Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstogo} \suchthat θ ∉ (¬\OF_C(\allleadstoo))^⊆ ⇔ \OF_D(\allleadstogo) ⇒ \OF_{D ∖ C}(\allleadstogo)$.
\end{proof}
\begin{corollary}
  Considering a claim $C$ and prior $D$ being $¬\OF_C(\allleadstoo)$,
  $D ∖ C$ is falsifiable iff $C$ satisfies established verifiability under prior $¬\OF_C(\allleadstogo)$.
\end{corollary}

    It follows, still by obs-compatibility, that for any $\hist'$, $\hist' \ileadstog p$ (otherwise pick $θ$ including $(\hist', i)$ and any $(\hist^{\prime\prime}, j)$). Conclude that $\allleadstog$ is constant to $p$.
    It follows that the obs-compatible relations are $\set{{\allleadstog^p}, {\allleadstog^{p'}}}$, thus, $C$ is falsifiable.
  \end{example}
  \begin{example}
    \label{ex:consti1}
    Assume that $\card{I} = 1$.
    Consider any $p, p' ∈ P$ and the constant functions ${\allleadstog^p}$ to $p$ and ${\allleadstog^{p'}}$ to $p'$ and the claim $C = \set{{\allleadstog^p}, {\allleadstog^{p'}}}$.
    We have $C^⊆ ∩ Θ = \set{θ \suchthat θ(θ^{-1}(P)) = \set{p} ∨ θ(θ^{-1}(P)) = \set{p'}}$.
    Consider any relation $\allleadstog$ that is obs-compatible with $C^⊆ ∩ Θ$. Assume that for some $\hist, i$, $\hist \ileadstog p$. Observe that by obs-compatibility, $∅ \ileadstog p$.
    It follows, still by obs-compatibility, that for any $\hist'$, $\hist' \ileadstog p$ (otherwise pick $θ$ including $(\hist', i)$ and $(∅, i)$). Conclude that $\allleadstog$ is constant to $p$.
    It follows that the obs-compatible relations are $\set{{\allleadstog^p}, {\allleadstog^{p'}}}$, thus, $C$ is falsifiable.
  \end{example}
  \begin{example}
    \label{ex:constwout0}
    Consider any $p, p' ∈ P$ and the claim $C = \set{{\allleadstog} \suchthat (∀ \hist ≠ ∅, i ∈ I: \hist \ileadstog p) ∨ (∀ \hist ≠ ∅, i ∈ I: \hist \ileadstog p')}$. That claim is falsifiable iff $\card I ≥ 2$ or $\card \allargs = 1$. The proof if so is similar to \cref{ex:consti2}; otherwise, pick any $\ar ∈ \allargs$ and note that any relation satisfying $(a.*) \ileadstog p$ and $∀ b ≠ a: (b.*) \ileadstog p'$ is obs-compatible with $C^⊆ ∩ Θ$ but is not in $C$.
  \end{example}
  \begin{example}
    \label{ex:unfor}
    Consider any $i$, $\hist, \histp ∈ \allhists$, $p, p' ∈ P$ where $\hist$ and $\histp$ are not prefix related.
    The claim $C = \set{{\allleadstog} \suchthat \hist \ileadstog p ∨ \histp \ileadstog p'}$ is unfalsifiable.

    We have $C^⊆ ∩ Θ = \set{θ \suchthat (θ(\hist, i) = ∅ ∨ θ(\hist, i) = p) ∨ (θ(\histp, i) = ∅ ∨ θ(\histp, i) = p')}$.
    Observe that any $θ ∈ Θ$ satisfy $θ(\hist, i) = ∅ ∨ θ(\histp, i) = ∅$, thus, $C^⊆ ∩ Θ = Θ$.
    It follows that the obs-compatible relations are $\allleadstos$, which is bigger than $C$.
  \end{example}
  \begin{example}
    Consider any $i$, $\ar ≠ \ar' ∈ \allargs$, $p, p' ∈ P$.
    The claim $C = \set{{\allleadstog} \suchthat (\ar) \ileadstog p ∨ (\ar') \ileadstog p'}$ is unfalsifiable (per \cref{ex:unfor}).

    Now consider the smaller claim $C' = \set{{\allleadstog} \suchthat (\ar_1) \ileadstog p}$ (thus $C' \ssub C$).
    It yields a smaller set ${C'}^⊆ ∩ Θ = \set{θ \suchthat (θ((\ar_1), i) = ∅ ∨ θ((\ar_1), i) = p)}$.
    The obs-compatible relations are the smaller set $\set{{\allleadstog} \suchthat (\ar_1) \ileadstog p}$.
    Thus, $C'$ is falsifiable.

    Note that any claim $C^{\prime\prime}$ bigger than $C$ is unfalsifiable as well, unless trivial:
    from $C ⊆ C^{\prime\prime}$, we deduce $Θ = C^⊆ ∩ Θ ⊆ {C^{\prime\prime}}^⊆ ∩ Θ$, thus ${C^{\prime\prime}}^⊆ ∩ Θ = Θ$ and the obs-compatible relations are $\allleadstos$, which equals $C^{\prime\prime}$ only if $C^{\prime\prime}$ is trivial.

    Now consider the alternative smaller claim of \cref{ex:consti2}, $C^{(2)} = \set{{\allleadstog^p}, {\allleadstog^{p'}}}$ (thus $C^{(2)} \ssub C$).
    It yields a smaller set ${C^{(2)}}^⊆ ∩ Θ$.
  \end{example}
  \begin{example}
    \label{ex:for}
    Consider any $i$ and $p, p' ∈ P$.
    The claim $C = \set{{\allleadstog} \suchthat (∀ \hist: \card{\hist} ∈ 2ℕ ⇒ \hist \ileadstog p) ∨ (∀ \hist: (\card{\hist} ∈ 2ℕ + 1 ∨ \hist = ∅) ⇒ \hist \ileadstog p')}$, namely, all paths of even lengths lead $i$ to $p$ OR all paths of odd lengths or empty lead $i$ to $p'$, is falsifiable.

    Note that $C^⊆ ∩ Θ = \set{θ ∈ Θ \suchthat (∀ \hist: \card{\hist} ∈ 2ℕ ⇒ θ(\hist) ∈ \set{∅, p}) ∨ (∀ \hist: (\card{\hist} ∈ 2ℕ + 1 ∨ \hist = ∅) ⇒ θ(\hist) ∈ \set{∅, p'})}$.
    Consider any obs-compatible ${\allleadstog}$.
    Assume that $∃ \hist \suchthat \card{\hist} ∈ 2ℕ ∧ \hist \nileadstog p$.
    Pick $θ = \set{(∅, i, {\ileadstog}(∅)), (\hist, i, {\ileadstog}(\hist))}$. We have $({\allleadstog} ∩ θ) ∈ C^⊆ ∩ Θ$, and ${\allleadstog} ∩ θ = θ$, and from $θ ∈ C^⊆ ∩ Θ$ we obtain ${\ileadstog}(∅) = p'$.
    Similarly, using $θ = \set{(∅, i, {\ileadstog}(∅)), (\histp, j, {\ileadstog[j]}(\histp))}$, we obtain that $\allleadsto$ conforms to the relation in $C$.
  \end{example}

  \begin{remark}
    Intuition (to be explored) is that when $C^⊆ ∩ Θ$ has the covering property, then the obs-compatible relations are easily deduced from the set (something like, just consider compatibility point per point, one singleton observation at a time). Covering is what is used above, something like $∃ i, \hist, \histp$ such that $∀ \hist^{\prime\prime}: [∀ j ≠ i: \set{(\hist, i, .), (\histp, j, .)} ∈ C^⊆ ∩ Θ] ∧ [∃ j ≠ i \suchthat \set{(\hist', i, .), (\hist^{\prime\prime}, j, .)} ∈ C^⊆ ∩ Θ]$.
  \end{remark}

\subsection{Compatibility of claim properties}
We are here interested in the interplay between properties of holding, anonymity, (strong) informativeness beyond counting, and falsifiability.
\Cref{th:hsif,th:hif,th:asif} show their compatibility when one of holding or anonymity is not required (except that informativeness beyond counting cannot be made strong in the edge case where $∀ i ∈ I: P_i = ∅$).

We thus turn to requiring both anonymous and holding.
They are always satisfiable when only these two are required (\cref{th:ha}).
\Cref{th:infoBeyonCKO} (and extensions, \cref{th:infoBeyonCKOext,th:strinfoBeyonCKO}) shows that informativeness beyond counting (and variants) is too demanding if one wants to investigate situations where individuals have differing deliberate preferences.

It is possible under reasonable conditions of regularity to satisfy anonymous and holding together with indicativeness. This happens for example when everybody has a deliberate judgment and a finite set of decisive arguments suffices to lead everybody to their deliberate judgment (\cref{th:suffIndicDec}).

  \begin{theorem}
    \label{th:hsif}
    For all exposure protocols, there exists a claim that holds and is strongly informative beyond counting and falsifiable iff $∃ i \suchthat P_i ≠ ∅$.
  \end{theorem}
  \begin{proof}
    Define $C = \set{\allleadsto}$.
  \end{proof}
  \begin{theorem}
    \label{th:hif}
    For all exposure protocols, there exists a claim that holds and is informative beyond counting and falsifiable.
  \end{theorem}
  \begin{proof}
    Define $C = \set{\allleadsto}$.
  \end{proof}
  \begin{theorem}
    \label{th:asif}
    For all exposure protocols, there exists a claim that is anonymous, strongly informative beyond counting and falsifiable.
  \end{theorem}
  \begin{proof}
    Define the claim as $∀ i ∈ I, \hist ∈ \allhists: \hist \ileadstog φ$.
  \end{proof}

  \begin{theorem}
    \label{th:ha}
    For all exposure protocols, there exists a claim that holds and is anonymous.
  \end{theorem}
  \begin{proof}
    Define the claim as $∀ i ∈ I: {\ileadstog} ∈ \set{{\ileadsto[j]} \suchthat j ∈ I}$.
  \end{proof}

  \begin{theorem}
    \label{th:HAinformativeness}
    Given any $Q ⊆ P ∪ \set{∅}$,
    the following two conditions on $\allleadsto$ are equivalent:
    there exists a claim that holds and is anonymous and satisfies
    $∃ j ∈ I \suchthat π(C)(j) \ssub Q$;
    and
    $\set{P_i, i ∈ I} \ssub Q$.
  \end{theorem}
  \begin{proof}
    Consider any claim $C$ that holds and is anonymous, pick any $j ∈ I$, and let us show that $\set{P_i, i ∈ I} ⊆ π(C)(j)$.
    Pick any $p ∈ \set{P_i, i ∈ I}$.
    Pick any $i ∈ I$ such that $π({\allleadsto})(i) = p$.
    Define $σ$ as the permutation exchanging $i$ and $j$.
    As $C$ holds, it contains $\allleadsto$, and by anonymity, it contains ${\allleadsto} ∘ σ$. Observing that $π({\allleadsto} ∘ σ)(j) = π({\allleadsto})(i) = p$, we obtain $p ∈ π(C)(j)$.

    It follows that for any claim $C$ that holds and is anonymous, if $∃ j ∈ I \suchthat π(C)(j) \ssub Q$ then $\set{P_i, i ∈ I} ⊆ π(C)(j) \ssub Q$ thus $\set{P_i, i ∈ I} \ssub Q$.

    Turning to the other direction, if $\set{P_i, i ∈ I} \ssub Q$, then pick any $p ∈ Q ∖ \set{P_i, i ∈ I}$; the claim $∀ i ∈ I: P_i ≠ p$ satisfies the desired properties.
  \end{proof}

  \begin{theorem}
    \label{th:infoBeyonCKO}
    There exists a claim that holds and is anonymous and informative beyond counting iff $\set{P_i, i ∈ I} ≠ P ∪ \set{∅}$.
  \end{theorem}
  \begin{proof}
    From \cref{th:HAinformativeness} with $Q = P ∪ \set{∅}$, we have that
    there exists a claim that holds and is anonymous and satisfies
    $∃ j ∈ I \suchthat π(C)(j) \ssub P ∪ \set{∅}$ iff
    $\set{P_i, i ∈ I} \ssub P ∪ \set{∅}$.
  \end{proof}

  \begin{theorem}
    \label{th:strinfoBeyonCKO}
    There exists a claim that holds and is anonymous and strongly informative beyond counting iff $\set{P_i, i ∈ I} \ssub P$.
  \end{theorem}
  \begin{proof}
    From \cref{th:HAinformativeness} with $Q = P$, we have that
    there exists a claim that holds and is anonymous and satisfies
    $∃ j ∈ I \suchthat π(C)(j) \ssub P$ iff
    $\set{P_i, i ∈ I} \ssub P$.
  \end{proof}

  \begin{theorem}
    \label{th:infoBeyonCKOext}
    Given $Q = \set{φ, φ'}$,
    the following two conditions on $\allleadsto$ are equivalent:
    there exists a claim that holds and is anonymous and satisfies
    $∃ j ∈ I \suchthat π(C)(j) \ssub Q$;
    and
    $\set{P_i, i ∈ I} \ssub Q$.
  \end{theorem}
  \begin{proof}
    From \cref{th:HAinformativeness} with $Q = \set{φ, φ'}$, we have that
    there exists a claim that holds and is anonymous and satisfies
    $∃ j ∈ I \suchthat π(C)(j) \ssub \set{φ, φ'}$ iff
    $\set{P_i, i ∈ I} \ssub \set{φ, φ'}$.
  \end{proof}

  Define the claim $C^\text{HA} = \set{{\allleadsto} ∘ σ \suchthat σ: I ⇔ I}$.
  Under anonymity, requesting that a claim holds is the same as requesting that it includes $C^\text{HA}$.
  \begin{theorem}
    \label{th:claim_ha}
    A claim holds and is anonymous iff it includes $C^\text{HA}$ and is anonymous.
    In particular, $C^\text{HA}$ holds and is anonymous.
  \end{theorem}
  \begin{remark}
    Define $C^\text{A} = \set{{\allleadstog} ∈ \allleadstos \suchthat {\allleadstog}(I) = {\allleadsto}(I)}$. Observe that $C^\text{A}$ is anonymous, that $C^\text{HA} ⊆ C^\text{A}$, and that $C^\text{HA} ≠ C^\text{A}$ iff $∃ i, j ∈ I: {\ileadsto} ≠ {\ileadsto[j]}$ (to be checked).
  \end{remark}

  \begin{theorem}
    \label{th:suffIndic}
    The following two conditions on $\allleadsto$ are equivalent:
    \begin{enumerate}[label=T\ref{th:suffIndic}-{\arabic*}]
      \item \label{it:condHAI} for some $H ∈ \finite(\incr(\allhists))$ and $i_1 ∈ I \suchthat P_{i_1} ∈ P$, we have $∀ i_2 \suchthat P_{i_2} ≠ P_{i_1}$ that $∃ \hist ∈ H \suchthat {\ileadsto[i_1]}(\hist) ≠ {\ileadsto[i_2]}(\hist)$;
      \item \label{it:HAI} there exists a claim that holds and is anonymous and indicative.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{theorem}
  \begin{proof}
    For \ref{it:condHAI} $⇒$ \ref{it:HAI},
    define $p = P_{i_1}$.
    Consider any claim $C$ such that $C ⊆ \set{{\allleadstog} ∈ \allleadstos \suchthat {\ileadsto[i_1]} ∈ {\allleadstog}(I) ⊆ {\allleadsto}(I)}$ and $C$ is anonymous (for example, $C^\text{HA}$ is suitable).
    % Define the claim $C$ as $\set{{\allleadstog} ∈ \allleadstos \suchthat ∀ i ∈ I: {\ileadstog} ∈ \set{{\ileadsto[j]} \suchthat j ∈ I}}$ (or as anything included in it that includes $C^\text{HA}$ and is anonymous).
    The claim is indicative for $i = i_1$ and $θ = \set{(\hist, i_1, {\ileadsto[i_1]}(\hist)) \suchthat \hist ∈ H}$ for the following reason.
    The attitudes in $C$ compatible with $θ$ are $\set{{\allleadstog} \suchthat θ ⊆ {\allleadstog}} ∩ C ⊆ \set{{\allleadstog} \suchthat {\ileadstog[i_1]} ∈ \set{{\ileadsto[j]} \suchthat P_j = p}}$, and $π(\set{{\allleadstog} \suchthat {\ileadstog[i_1]} ∈ \set{{\ileadsto[j]} \suchthat P_j = p}})(i_1) = p$.

    For \cref{it:HAI} $⇒$ \cref{it:condHAI},
    pick an indicative claim $C$ and consider $i ∈ I$ and $θ ∈ Θ ∩ (\allhists × \set{i} × P) ∩ C^⊆$ such that $π(\set{{\allleadstog} \suchthat θ ⊆ {\allleadstog}} ∩ C)(i) \ssub P$.
    Define $H = θ^{-1}(i, P)$.
  \end{proof}
  \begin{corollary}
    \label{th:suffIndicDec}
    Assume that for some $p ∈ P$, we have $∃ i ∈ I \suchthat P_i = p$ and $∀ i_2 \suchthat P_{i_2} ≠ p$ we have that $∃ A ∈ \finite(\allargs) \suchthat ∀ i ∈ I \suchthat P_i ≠ p, ∃ a ∈ A \suchthat {\ileadstost}(a) ∈ P ∖ \set{p}$, then there exists a claim that holds and is anonymous and indicative.
  \end{corollary}
  \begin{proof}
    We know that $i_1$ has some decisive argument $a_1$ in favor of $p$.
    Assume (wlog) that $A = \set{2, …, \card{A} + 1}$.
    Define $\hist_1 = ∅$ and for $i ∈ \intvl{2, \card{A} + 1}$, $\hist^{(i)} = (\hist_{i - 1}, a_1, i)$.
    Define $H = \set{\hist^{(i)} \suchthat i ∈ \intvl{2, \card{A} + 1}}$.
    Apply \cref{th:suffIndic}.
  \end{proof}
  \begin{example}
    Consider $I_p$ such that $∀ i ∈ I_p$: $i$ constantly says $p$.
    Consider $J = I ∖ I_p$ whose DJ is $p' ≠ p$, and such that each $j ∈ J$ has a single decisive argument $\ar_j$ for $p'$, and when $\ar_j$ is not in $\hist$, $\hist \ileadsto[j] p$.
    Then, the condition is not satisfied.
  \end{example}
  \begin{theorem}
    \label{th:caractNotIndic}
    The following conditions on $\allleadsto$ are equivalent:
    \begin{enumerate}[label=T\ref{th:caractNotIndic}-{\arabic*}]
      \item \label{it:NotHAI} there exists no claim that holds and is anonymous and indicative;
      \item \label{it:condNotHAI} all paths are such that for all $i_1$ having a DJ, there is an $i_2$ with another deliberated judgement that behaves identically on the whole path (formally: $∀ \hist ∈ \allhists, i_1 ∈ I \suchthat π(i_1) ≠ ∅: ∃ i_2 ∈ I \suchthat π(i_1) ≠ π(i_2) ∧ ∀ \histp ⊆ \hist: {\ileadsto[i_1]}(\histp) = {\ileadsto[i_2]}(\histp)$);
      \item \label{it:condNotHAIInf} all paths are such that for all $i_1$, either at least one individual without a DJ, or infinitely many individuals $i_2$ with another deliberated judgement than $i_1$, behave identically to $i_1$ on the whole path (formally: $∀ \hist ∈ \allhists, i_1 ∈ I: [∃ i_2 ∈ I \suchthat π(i_2) = ∅ ∧ ∀ \histp ⊆ \hist: {\ileadsto[i_1]}(\histp) = {\ileadsto[i_2]}(\histp)] ∨ [\card(\set{i_2 ∈ I \suchthat π(i_1) ≠ π(i_2) ∧ ∀ \histp ⊆ \hist: {\ileadsto[i_1]}(\histp) = {\ileadsto[i_2]}(\histp)}) = ∞]$)
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{theorem}
  \begin{proof}
    Let us show that NOT \ref{it:condNotHAI} implies \ref{it:condHAI}.
    Consider: some individual $i$ and some path $\hist$ are such that $π(i) ≠ ∅$ and
    $∀ i_2 ∈ I \suchthat π(i) ≠ π(i_2): ∃ \histp ⊆ \hist \suchthat {\ileadsto[i]}(\histp) ≠ {\ileadsto[i_2]}(\histp)$.
    Define $H = \set{\histp ⊆ \hist}$ and $i_1 = i$.

    Conversely, \ref{it:condHAI} implies NOT \ref{it:condNotHAI}.
    Consider: for some $H ∈ \finite(\incr(\allhists))$ and $i ∈ I \suchthat P_{i} ∈ P$, we have $∀ i_2 \suchthat P_{i_2} ≠ P_i$ that $∃ \histp ∈ H \suchthat {\ileadsto}(\histp) ≠ {\ileadsto[i_2]}(\histp)$.
    Define $\hist$ as the maximal path (in cardinal) in $H$.

    Let us now prove that \ref{it:condNotHAI} implies \ref{it:condNotHAIInf}.
    Consider any $\hist ∈ \allhists, i_1 ∈ I$.
    Define $J = \set{i_2 ∈ I \suchthat ∀ \histp ⊆ \hist: {\ileadsto[i_1]}(\histp) = {\ileadsto[i_2]}(\histp)}$.
    Assume that $∀ i_2 ∈ J: π(i_2) ≠ ∅$ (otherwise, the first disjunct of \ref{it:condNotHAIInf} holds), and let us show that $\card\set{i_2 ∈ J \suchthat π(i_1) ≠ π(i_2)} = ∞$.
    From $∀ i_2 ∈ J: π(i_2) ≠ ∅$, we obtain $π(i_1) ≠ ∅$, thus, $∃ a_{i_1} ∈ \allargs \suchthat a_{i_1} \ileadstost[i_1] π(i_1)$.
    Apply \ref{it:condNotHAI} to obtain some $i_2 ∈ J$ such that $π(i_1) ≠ π(i_2)$.
    Also, by our assumption, $π(i_2) ≠ ∅$, thus, $∃ a_{i_2} ∈ \allargs \suchthat a_{i_2} \ileadstost[i_2] π(i_2)$.

\subsection{Theories using a static decisive argument}
  \label{sec:static}
  \Cref{ex:tutor} suggests a simple form for theories of deliberated choice: suffice to determine an argument $\ar ∈ \allargs$ and a preference $p ∈ P$ such that the theory claims that $∀ i ∈ I: \ar \ileadstost p$ and that $∀ i ∈ I: p ∈ P_i$.

  \begin{definition}[Static theory]
    \label{def:static}
    A static theory is a pair $(p, \ar)$ for some $p ∈ P$ and some $\ar ∈ \allargs$.
  \end{definition}
  A static theory $(p, \ar)$ is said to be truthful iff $∀ i ∈ I: \ar \ileadstost p$, and to be correct iff $∀ i ∈ I: p ∈ P_i$.
  We can then deduce that such theories are valid, meaning that their truthfulness implies their correctness. Let us make this (very simple) result stand out as a theorem, because a parallel will be usefully made with more complicated theories.
  %Note that this result immediately follows from \cref{ax:decisive}.
  \begin{theorem}[Static theories are valid]
    Any truthful static theory is correct.
  \end{theorem}
  \begin{proof}
    This follows immediately from \cref{def:decisive}.
  \end{proof}
  Importantly, truthfulness can indeed be checked using our observational basis, that is, if $∃ i ∈ I \suchthat \ar \nileadstost p$, then it is possible to exhibit this fact experimentally.

  Such theories have the merit of simplicity, but there is a need to design more adaptive theories: in many interesting cases, no single argument will be considered decisive by all individuals in $I$. We then need theories that argue with individuals by reacting to what they consider good arguments. The following section illustrates this need.

  \subsection{Some decision problems with no single decisive argument}
  \label{sec:lichtenstein}
  One ambition of the theories to be proposed here is to permit exhibiting the existence of a deliberated consensus among a large set of persons about issues that involve numerous arguments and counter-arguments.

  In many decision problems, individuals will not be attracted by the same set of arguments and counter-arguments, even though they may finally settle to the same preference. Imagine that some individual $i_1$ considers an argument $a_1$ for $φ'$ relevant and some individual $i_2$ considers an argument $a_2$ for $φ'$ relevant, but $i_1$ does not consider $a_2$ relevant and $i_2$ does not consider $a_1$ relevant (for example, because they already have some counter-arguments in mind to those arguments). Imagine further that there is some counter-argument $a'_1$ that can be given to $i_1$ to rebut $a_1$, and a different counter-argument $a'_2$ that can be given to $i_2$ to rebut $a_2$, so that they both have $φ$ in their deliberated judgment when considering all these arguments.
  In such situation, static theories as defined in \cref{sec:static} are ill suited: these theories need to present the same argument to all individuals and would thus need to define a bigger argument $a$ containing both $a'_1$ and $a'_2$. Such an argument could be too long to be convincing to $i_1$ or $i_2$, as it needlessly spends efforts countering an argument that the individual considers irrelevant to start with. This  will become an important problem if there are not only two but many a priori possible arguments $a_k$ for $φ'$, a small subset of which is considered relevant by any given individual, each with their own counter-argument $a'_k$ for $φ$.
  This section is devoted to describing two examples to illustrate that point. Bear in mind that these examples may look somehow naïve on the aspect of the hypothesis they make about what convinces individuals. Real-world complex decision situations will require complex theories that do not fit the purpose of simple illustrative examples. Their goal is to argue that non-static theories are required, but not to fully describe such theories.

It may also be that, for each individual who considers relevant some arguments $A$ against the proposed concrete action, it is possible to give counter-arguments $A'$ that will convince the individual to opt for the proposal.
  One of the settings discussed by \citet{thaler_nudge_2009} permits such an illustration. %to illustrate this point more concretely.
  Let $I$ be a set of workers interested in establishing a savings plan.
  The authors claim that workers tend to not save enough because of psychological biases.
  It is therefore tempting to conjecture that the deliberated choice of those workers is to save more, in the sense defined here, than what their natural choice (the one revealed in absence of arguments) leads to.
  A difficulty related to this decision problem is that the complete set of all arguments that will be considered relevant by at least one individual for such a complex issue may well be very large. It may also be that, for each individual who considers relevant some arguments $A$ for saving not much (i.e., spending more in the short term), it is possible to give counter-arguments $A'$ that will convince the individual to opt for saving more.
  But no single argument could be decisive for each $i ∈ I$: it could be that only counter-arguments built specifically according to what a given individual considers relevant could convince her, given the complexity of the issue.
  This illustrates that the deliberated choice of each individuals in $I$ may be to save more even though no static theory (\cref{def:static}) may achieve showing it, as it could be that no such theory is truthful.

  \begin{example}[A decision problem about two lotteries]

    Define a counter-argument $\ar_l$ to $\ar_u$, indicating that for small monetary amounts, it is perfectly reasonable to consider utility as linear, especially given that in this experiment, multiple lotteries will be played.%

  \section{Technical notes about falsification}
  \begin{definition}
    \label{def:unfals}
    A claim is \emph{unfalsifiable} iff
    $C = {\allleadstos} ∨
      ∃ {\allleadstog} ∉ C \suchthat Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstog} ⊆ C^⊆$.
  \end{definition}

  A claim $C$ is \emph{falsifiable} iff $∀ {\allleadstog} ∉ C: ∃ θ ⊆ {\allleadstog} \suchthat θ ∉ C^⊆$,
  equivalently iff $∃ {\allleadstog} ∉ C \suchthat ∀ θ ∈ Θ, θ ⊆ {\allleadstog}: θ ∈ C^⊆$.
  (To be corrected for the trivial claims.)

  \subsection{Comparison with rejection}
  The following theorem (whose condition is that $C$ may fail to hold and still be not rejectable) is a consequence of the contrapositive of \cref{th:holdsiff}.
  \begin{corollary}
    \label{th:holdsiffCorr}
    A claim $C$ that satisfies $∃ {\allleadstog} ∉ C$ such that $C$ is not rejectable given $\allleadstog$ is unfalsifiable and not trivial.
  \end{corollary}
  The following theorem (an immediate consequence of \cref{def:unfals}) is the converse of \cref{th:holdsiffCorr}.
  It shows that falsifiability or triviality is exactly the property that we need to fill the gap.
  \begin{theorem}
    \label{th:holdsiffConv}
    For any unfalsifiable and not trivial claim $C$:
    $∃ {\allleadstog} ∉ C$ such that $C$ is not rejectable given $\allleadstog$.
  \end{theorem}
  \begin{remark}
    It is interesting to note that strong unfalsifiability entails necessary non rejection whereas strict unfalsifiability entails possible non rejection. Perhaps we should exploit this by showing that a given relation not in C entails non rejection using that relation, or something like that?
  \end{remark}

\begin{remark}
  \label{rq:precise}
  Making a strongly unfalsifiable claim more precise ($C = (C_1 ∩ C_2) ⊆ C_1$ in \cref{th:strongUToStrictU}) can make it strictly unfalsifiable.
  Also, a strictly unfalsifiable claim can be made falsifiable by picking a single relation from the claim.

\end{remark}

\subsection{Coverability}
  \begin{definition}
    \label{def:covr}
    A claim $C$ is \emph{coverable} iff $∀ θ ∈ Θ ∩ C^⊆: ∃ θ' ∈ Θ ∩ C^⊆ \suchthat θ ⊆ θ' ∧ \set{{\allleadstog} ∈ \allleadstos \suchthat θ' ∈ \powerset{\allleadstog}} ⊆ C$.
  \end{definition}

  \begin{theorem}
    \label{th:extStronglyU}
    For any coverable claim $C$ and strongly unfalsifiable claim $C'$:
    $Θ ∩ C^⊆ ⊆ (C' ∩ C)^⊆$.
  \end{theorem}
  \begin{proof}
    Consider any $θ ∈ Θ ∩ C^⊆$.
    Let us show that $θ ∈ ∪_{{\allleadstog} ∈ (C' ∩ C)} \powerset{\allleadstog}$.

    Consider any $θ' ∈ Θ ∩ C^⊆ \suchthat θ ⊆ θ' ∧ \set{{\allleadstog} ∈ \allleadstos \suchthat θ' ∈ \powerset{\allleadstog}} ⊆ C$.
    As $Θ ⊆ {C'}^⊆$, we have $θ' ∈ ∪_{{\allleadstog} ∈ C'} \powerset{\allleadstog}$.
    Pick any such $\allleadstog$ (thus with $θ' ∈ \powerset{\allleadstog}$ and ${\allleadstog} ∈ C'$). Thus, $θ ∈ \powerset{\allleadstog}$.
    Also, as $\set{{\allleadstog} ∈ \allleadstos \suchthat θ' ∈ \powerset{\allleadstog}} ⊆ C$, we have ${\allleadstog} ∈ C$.
  \end{proof}
  A claim $C$ is not coverable iff $∃ θ ∈ Θ ∩ C^⊆ \suchthat ∀ θ' ∈ Θ ∩ C^⊆, θ ⊆ θ': ∃ {\allleadstog} ∉ C \suchthat θ' ∈ \powerset{\allleadstog}$.

  A claim $C$ is not coverable because of the empty set iff $∀ θ ∈ Θ ∩ C^⊆: ∃ {\allleadstog} ∉ C \suchthat θ ∈ \powerset{\allleadstog}$.

  \begin{remark}
    We do not have that when $C_1 ∩ C_2 ≠ ∅$, then $θ ∈ C_1^⊆ ∧ θ ∈ C_2^⊆ ⇒ θ ∈ (C_1 ∩ C_2)^⊆$.
    For a counter-example, consider $\hist, \hist_1, \hist_2 ∈ \allhists$, $i, i_1, i_2 ∈ I$ and $p ≠ p', p_1, p_2 ∈ P$ and $C_1 = \set{{\allleadstog} ∈ \allleadstos \suchthat (\hist_1 \ileadstog[i_1] p_1 ⇒ \hist \ileadstog p) ∧ (\hist_2 \ileadstog[i_2] p_2 ⇒ \hist \ileadstog p')}$, $C_2 = \set{{\allleadstog} ∈ \allleadstos \suchthat (\hist \ileadstog p ⇒ \hist_2 \ileadstog[i_2] p_2)}$ and $θ = \set{(\hist_1, i_1, p_1)}$.
  \end{remark}
  \begin{remark}
    The above remark extends to the case where $C_1$ is strongly unfalsifiable.
    For a counter-example, consider $C_1 = \set{{\allleadstog} ∈ \allleadstos \suchthat (\ar) \ileadstog φ ∨ (\ar' \ileadstog φ)}$ and $C_2$ containing a condition requiring $(\ar) \nileadstog φ ∧ (\ar' \nileadstog φ)$.
    Add implications if desired to make $C_1$ and $C_2$ partially compatible, for example, $C_1 = \set{{\allleadstog} ∈ \allleadstos \suchthat (α \ileadstog[j] p) ⇒ [(\ar) \ileadstog φ ∨ (\ar' \ileadstog φ)]}$, so that $C_1$ and $C_2$ require their condition only when some trigger holds.
    Note that the $C_2$ condition may be arbitrarily complex so I see no hope of caracterizing these situations.
  \end{remark}

  \end{remark}

  \subsection{Closure under undistinguishability}
  The following procedure permits to determine whether a claim is falsifiable.
  Given a set of observations $S ⊆ Θ$, define the relations that are observation-compatible with $S$ as $E(S) = \set{{\allleadstog} ∈ \allleadstos \suchthat ∀ θ ∈ Θ: {\allleadstog} ∩ θ ∈ S}$.
  Given a claim $C$, define the set of its observation-compatible relations as $E(C) = E(C^⊆ ∩ Θ)$ (reusing the letter $E$ by abuse of notation).
  Note that this set extends $C$, in the sense that $C ⊆ E(C)$.
  The claim $C$ is falsifiable iff it is closed under extension to observation-compatible relations, that is, iff $E(C) ⊆ C$.
  \begin{theorem}
    \label{thm:caractFals}
    A claim $C$ is falsifiable or trivial iff $E(C) ⊆ C$, or equivalently, iff
    $∀ {\allleadstog} ∈ \allleadstos$:
    \[\set{\restr{\allleadstog}{O} \suchthat O ∈ \allpriobss} ⊆ C^⊆ ⇒ {\allleadstog} ∈ C.\]
  \end{theorem}
  \begin{proof}
    Note that
    $E(C) = E(C^⊆ ∩ Θ) = \set{{\allleadstog} ∈ \allleadstos \suchthat ∀ O ∈ \allpriobss: \restr{\allleadstog}{O} ∈ C^⊆}$.
  
    The condition $E(C) ⊆ C$ is equivalent to
    $∀ {\allleadstog} ∈ \allleadstos: ({\allleadstog} ∈ E(C) ⇒ {\allleadstog} ∈ C)$.
    Note that ${\allleadstog} ∈ E(C) ⇔ \set{\restr{\allleadstog}{O} \suchthat O ∈ \allpriobss} ⊆ C^⊆$.
  
    By definition, $C$ is not falsifiable iff $∃ {\allleadstog} ∉ C \suchthat Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstog} ⊆ C^⊆$;
    equivalently, iff $∃ {\allleadstog} ∉ C \suchthat \set{θ ⊆ {\allleadstog}} ⊆ C^⊆$.
    And, $∀ {\allleadstog} ∈ \allleadstos$: $\set{\restr{\allleadstog}{O} \suchthat O ∈ \allpriobss} = \set{θ ⊆ {\allleadstog}}$.
  \end{proof}
  
  Let $S_θ = \set{{\allleadstog} ∈ \allleadstos \suchthat θ ⊆ {\allleadstog}}$ denote the possibilities for $\allleadsto$ compatible with the observations.
  Given $O$ and ${\allleadstog}$, it follows that $S_{\restr{\allleadstog}{O_O}}$ denotes the “leads to” relations that complete $O_O$ using $\allleadstog$.
  Define that a relation ${\allleadstog} ∈ {\allleadstos}$ is \emph{obs-compatible} with a set $S$ iff $∀ θ ∈ Θ: ({\allleadstog} ∩ θ) ∈ S$.
  
  \begin{remark}
    \label{rq:closedcomb}
    Define the closure under combinations of $C$ as the relations that can be formed by picking parts of relations in $C$, formally, $(∪_{S ∈ C^⊆ ∩ Θ} S) ∩ \allleadstos$.
    We have $E(C) ⊆ (∪_{S ∈ C^⊆ ∩ Θ} S) ∩ \allleadstos$.
  
    Being closed under combinations is sufficient to be falsifiable, but not necessary, as seen with \cref{ex:consti2} (in other words, for some claims, $(∪_{S ∈ C^⊆ ∩ Θ} S) ∩ \allleadstos ⊆ E(C)$ does not hold).
  \end{remark}

\subsection{Another closure}
Given a set of attitudes $S ⊆ P^{\allhists}$, the claim associated to it designates the claim $S^I = \set{{\allleadstog} ∈ \allleadstos \suchthat ∀ i ∈ I: {\ileadstog} ∈ S}$.
Say that a claim $C$ is a claim about types iff $∃ S ⊆ P^{\allhists} \suchthat C = S^I$.
Informally, such a claim claims that the set of attitudes in $I$ is contained in $S$ (though might be a strict subset of $S$), or equivalently, that no attitude out of $S$ exists among $I$.

The following definition assumes a set $B ⊆ P^{\allhists}$ of behaviors. We take $B = P^{\allhists}$ if nothing is specified about $B$.
\begin{definition}
  Say that an attitude $g ∈ B$ is observably included in a set of attitudes $S ⊆ B$ iff $∀ H ∈ \finite(\incr(\allhists)): ∃ f ∈ S \suchthat \restr{g}{H} = \restr{f}{H}$.

  Define the extension of a set of attitudes $S ⊆ B$ to its observably included attitudes as the set of attitudes $R(S) = \set{g ∈ B \suchthat ∀ H ∈ \finite(\incr(\allhists)): ∃ f ∈ S \suchthat \restr{g}{H} = \restr{f}{H}}$.
  Note that $S ⊆ R(S)$.
  Also note that $S_1 ⊆ S_2 ⇒ R(S_1) ⊆ R(S_2)$.
\end{definition}
\begin{theorem}
  \label{th:RR}
  $∀ S ⊆ B: R(R(S)) ⊆ R(S)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  Consider any $g ∈ R(R(S))$.
  Consider any $H ∈ \finite(\incr(\allhists))$.
  There exists $f ∈ R(S)$ such that $\restr{g}{H} = \restr{f}{H}$.
  There exists $f' ∈ S$ such that $\restr{f}{H} = \restr{f'}{H}$.
  Thus, $\restr{g}{H} = \restr{f'}{H}$.
  Thus, $g ∈ R(S)$.
\end{proof}
Define the closure under observable inclusion of a claim about types $S$ as $R^∞(S)$, equivalently, as the smallest set $S'$ such that $S ⊆ S' = R(S')$, equivalently, as indicated by \cref{th:RR}, as $R(S)$.

Let $\intvl{\hist} = (\hist_i)_{i ∈ \intvl{0, \card{\hist}}} ∈ \finite(\incr(\allhists))$ denote the increasing sequence of paths from $∅$ to $\hist$.
\begin{theorem}
  \label{th:caractR}
  The $S$-labeling function associated to an attitude $f ∈ P^{\allhists}$ is the function $l^S_f: \allhists → \powerset{P^{\allhists}}$ defined as $l^S_f(\hist) = \set{g ∈ S \suchthat g(\hist) = f(\hist)}$.

  We have: $R(S) = \set{f \suchthat ∀ \hist ∈ \allhists: ∩_{\histp ⊆ \hist} l^S_f(\histp) ≠ ∅}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  Consider $f$ with $∀ \hist ∈ \allhists: ∩_{\histp ⊆ \hist} l^S_f(\histp) ≠ ∅$.
  Consider any $H ∈ \finite(\incr(\allhists))$.
  We know that $∩_{\histp ⊆ \hist}{l^S_f(\histp)} ≠ ∅$, thus in particular, defining $\hist = \max H$ and observing that $H ⊆ \set{\histp \suchthat \histp ⊆ \hist}$, we have that $∩_{\histp ∈ H} l^S_f(\histp) ≠ ∅$.
  Pick any $g ∈ ∩_{\histp ∈ H} l^S_f(\histp)$. Thus, $g ∈ S$.
  Also, $∀ \hist ∈ H: g ∈ l^S_f(\hist)$, thus,
  $∀ \hist ∈ H: g(\hist) = f(\hist)$.

  First observe that given any $S ⊆ P^{\allhists}$, $f ∈ P^{\allhists}$ and $\hist ∈ \allhists$, as $\histp ⊆ \hist$ iff $\histp ∈ \intvl{\hist}$, we have that $∩_{\histp ⊆ \hist} l^S_f(\histp) = \set{g ∈ S \suchthat ∀ \histp ∈ \intvl{\hist}: g(\histp) = f(\histp)}$.

  It follows that if $∀ \hist ∈ \allhists: ∩_{\histp ⊆ \hist} l^S_f(\histp) ≠ ∅$, then $∀ H ∈ \finite(\incr(\allhists))$: $∅ ≠ \set{g ∈ S \suchthat ∀ \histp ∈ \intvl{\max H}: g(\histp) = f(\histp)} ⊆ \set{g ∈ S \suchthat ∀ \histp ∈ H: g(\histp) = f(\histp)}$.

  Conversely, if $∀ H ∈ \finite(\incr(\allhists)): \set{g ∈ S \suchthat ∀ \histp ∈ H: g(\histp) = f(\histp)} ≠ ∅$, then $∀ \hist ∈ \allhists$, defining $H = \intvl{\hist}$, we have that $∩_{\histp ⊆ \hist} l^S_f(\histp) ≠ ∅$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
  \label{th:typesfalsifiable}
  A set of attitudes $S ⊆ P^{\allhists}$
  satisfies $∀ g ∈ P^{\allhists} ∖ S: ∃ H ∈ \finite(\incr(\allhists)) \suchthat ∀ f ∈ S: \restr{g}{H} ≠ \restr{f}{H}$,
  iff its associated claim is falsifiable or trivial.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Pick any ${\allleadstog} ∉ C$.
  By definition of $C$,
  $∃ i ∈ I \suchthat {{\ileadstog}} ∉ S$.
  As $C$ holds, ${\ileadstog} ∉ \set{{\ileadsto[j]} \suchthat j ∈ I}$ (\cref{th:typesholds}).
  Pick any $H$ such that $∀ {\ileadstog[j]} ∈ S: ∃ \hist ∈ H \suchthat {\ileadstog}(\hist) ≠ {\ileadstog[j]}(\hist)$.
  Define $θ = \set{(\hist, i, {\ileadstog}(\hist)) \suchthat \hist ∈ H} ∈ Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstog}$.
  Then, $θ ∉ C^⊆$, because $∀ {\ileadstog[j]} ∈ S: θ ⊈ {\ileadstog[j]}$.

  Conversely, assume that for some ${\ileadstog} ∈ P^{\allhists} ∖ S$, no $H ∈ \finite(\incr(\allhists))$ satisfies the indicated condition.
  Define ${\allleadstog}$ as including ${\ileadstog}$ and for all other individuals, relations from $S$.
  Then, ${\allleadstog} ∉ C$, but any observation will be in $C$, thus the claim is not falsifiable and not trivial.
\end{proof}
\begin{lemma}
  \label{th:typesholds}
  Consider a set of attitudes $S ⊆ P^{\allhists}$. Its associated claim holds iff $\set{{\ileadsto[j]} \suchthat j ∈ I} ⊆ S$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{lemma}
  \label{th:typesanonymous}
  Given any set of attitudes $S ⊆ P^{\allhists}$, its associated claim is anonymous.
\end{lemma}
The following result follows from \cref{th:typesfalsifiable,th:typesholds,th:typesanonymous}.
\begin{theorem}
  \label{th:suffFals}
  Assume that $∀ {\ileadstog} ∈ P^{\allhists} ∖ \set{{\ileadsto[k]} \suchthat k ∈ I}: ∃ H ∈ \finite(\incr(\allhists)) \suchthat ∀ j ∈ I: ∃ \hist ∈ H \suchthat {\ileadstog}(\hist) ≠ {\ileadsto[j]}(\hist)$.
  Then, the claim $C$ associated to the set of attitudes $S = \set{{\ileadsto[j]} \suchthat j ∈ I}$ holds, is anonymous, and is falsifiable or trivial.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  For another proof, note that $C^⊆ ∩ Θ = \set{θ ∈ Θ \suchthat ∀ i ∈ I: ∃ j ∈ I \suchthat θ ∩ (\allhists × \set{i} × P) ⊆ {\ileadsto[j]}}$.
  Pick any ${\allleadstog} ∈ E(C^⊆ ∩ Θ)$ and let us show that ${\allleadstog} ∈ C$.
  Note that ${\allleadstog} ∉ C ⇔ ∃ i ∈ I \suchthat {\ileadstog} ∈ P^{\allhists} ∖ \set{{\ileadsto[k]} \suchthat k ∈ I}$, in which case the consequent in the theorem holds.
  We need to show that $∄ i ∈ I, H ∈ \finite(\incr(\allhists)) \suchthat ∀ j ∈ I: ∃ \hist ∈ H \suchthat {\ileadstog}(\hist) ≠ {\ileadsto[j]}(\hist)$.
  In other words, given any $i ∈ I$, we need to show that $∀ H ∈ \finite(\incr(\allhists)): ∃ j ∈ I \suchthat ∀ \hist ∈ H: {\ileadstog}(\hist) = {\ileadsto[j]}(\hist)$.
\end{proof}

The following result follows from \cref{th:typesfalsifiable}.
\begin{theorem}
  If a set of attitudes $S ⊆ P^{\allhists}$ is not closed under observable inclusion, then it is not falsifiable and not trivial.
\end{theorem}
\begin{theorem}
  Given any situation, if the claim associated to the set of attitudes $R({\allleadsto}(I))$ is trivial, then there exists no claim associated to a set of attitudes that holds and is falsifiable.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  Consider a claim related to a set of attitudes $S$ that holds and that is falsifiable or trivial.
  For it to hold, we need ${\allleadsto}(I) ⊆ S$.
  From \cref{th:typesfalsifiable}, for it to be falsifiable or trivial, we need that $∀ g ∈ P^{\allhists} ∖ S: ∃ H ∈ \finite(\incr(\allhists)) \suchthat ∀ f ∈ S: \restr{g}{H} ≠ \restr{f}{H}$. In other words, all $g$ must be observably excludable from $S$. In other words, $S$ must be closed under observable inclusion. Thus, we need $R(S) ⊆ S$.
  Also, because $S_1 ⊆ S_2 ⇒ R(S_1) ⊆ R(S_2)$, we need
  $R({\allleadsto}(I)) ⊆ R(S) ⊆ S$.
  It follows that $S$ is trivial.
\end{proof}

  Given a set of attitudes $S$ and $\hist ∈ \allhists$, define the equivalence relation $=_\hist$ on $S$ as $f =_\hist g$ iff $f(\hist) = g(\hist)$. This also defines the quotient set $S / =_\hist$. \end{remark}

\begin{theorem}
  Given a claim $C$, define $S_C ⊆ P^{\allhists}$ as $S_C = ⋃_{{\allleadstog} ∈ C}({\allleadstog}(I))$.
  A claim $C$ is about types iff $C = (S_C)^I$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  If the consequent holds, then $C$ is about types, by definition.
  If a claim is about types $S$, then consider any $f ∈ S$, we have $C = S^I$ thus for ${\allleadstog}$ defined as ${\allleadstog}(I) = \set{f}$ we have ${\allleadstog} ∈ C$, thus $f ∈ S_C$; and consider any $f ∈ S_C$, consider any ${\allleadstog} ∈ C \suchthat f ∈ {\allleadstog}(I)$, we have ${\allleadstog} ∈ S^I$, thus $f ∈ S$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}
  \label{th:falsclosed}
  Under any $B ⊆ P^{\allhists}$ (thus recall $R(S) ⊆ B$), for any anonymous claim $C ⊆ B^I$ that is falsifiable or trivial, 
  $∀ {\allleadstog} ∈ C, F ⊆ {\allleadstog}(I) \suchthat [∀ f ∈ F: \card{({\allleadstoginv}(f))} ∉ ℕ]: R(F)^I ⊆ C$.

  $∃ {\allleadstog} ∈ C, ∅ ≠ F ⊆ {\allleadstog}(I) \suchthat ∀ f ∈ F: \card{({\allleadstoginv}(f))} ∉ ℕ$.
  Then, $R(F)^I ⊆ C$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  Consider any anonymous claim $C$, any ${\allleadstog} ∈ C, F ⊆ {\allleadstog}(I) \suchthat [∀ f ∈ F: \card{({\allleadstoginv}(f))} ∉ ℕ]$ and any ${\allleadstog'} ∈ R(F)^I ∖ C$.
  To show that $C$ is not falsifiable,
  consider any observable $θ ⊆ {\allleadstog'}$ and let us show that it is completable in $C$. Let $θ(P^{\allhists})$ denote the (finite) set of individuals that $θ$ interrogates.
  
  From the antecedent we see that $I$ is infinite.
  We know that $∀ i ∈ θ(P^{\allhists}): ∃ g ∈ R(F) \suchthat θ(i) ⊆ g$, thus, by definition of $R$, $∃ f ∈ F \suchthat θ(i) ⊆ f$.
  Define $γ: θ(P^{\allhists}) → F$ that picks one such $f$ for each $i$.
  Thus, $(γ^{-1}(f), f ∈ F)$ is a partition of $θ(P^{\allhists})$ such that $∀ i ∈ γ^{-1}(f): θ(i) ⊆ f$.

  Consider sets of same sizes $I'_f$ among the non observed individuals (meaning those in $I ∖ θ(P^{\allhists})$) and with corresponding types in ${\allleadstog}$ (so that $∀ f ∈ F, i ∈ I'_f: {\ileadstog} = f$).
  Define a bijection $σ$ that for each $f ∈ F$ bijects $I_f$ to $I'_f$ and that sends the elements of $I ∖ (∪_f I_f ∪ I'_f)$ to themselves.
  The relation ${\allleadstog} ∘ σ$ is in $C$ and is compatible with the observable.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}
  \label{th:AHF}
  The two following statements are equivalent.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $∀ \hist: P^{\intvl{\hist}} ⊆ \restr{{\allleadsto}(I)}{\intvl{\hist}}$
    \item There exists no anonymous claim that holds and is falsifiable.
  \end{itemize}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  If the first one is false, then the claim $R({\allleadsto}(I))$ is not trivial, thus is falsifiable.

  If the first one holds, let us show that starting from $C^\text{HA}$ and integrating all relations that are not observably distinguishable, we obtain the trivial claim.

  Consider any observable whatsoever, $O ∈ \allpriobss$, and let us show that for some bijection $σ$ over $I$, $O ⊆ ({\allleadsto} ∘ σ)$.

  For each of the (finitely many) individuals that $O$ contains, we can find a different behavior $f_i$ that is in $\restr{{\allleadsto}(I)}{\intvl{\hist}}$, thus, with some $j_i ∈ I \suchthat {\allleadsto}(j_i) = f_i$, and that is compatible with what $O$ sees of $i$. We define $σ$ so that it maps each of those individuals $i$ to the individual $j_i$ with the corresponding behavior.
  The resulting relation ${\allleadsto} ∘ σ$ is compatible with the observable.

  It follows that if the claim is not trivial, it is not falsifiable: pick any relation missing in the claim; it cannot be rejected by any observable.
\end{proof}
\begin{theorem}
  The following statements are equivalent.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $∃ \hist ∈ \allhists, f ∈ P^{\intvl{\hist}} \suchthat f ∉ \restr{{\allleadsto}(I)}{\intvl{\hist}}$.
    \item $∃ ∅ ≠ H ∈ \incr(\allhists), f ∈ P^H \suchthat f ∉ \restr{{\allleadsto}(I)}{H}$.
    \item There exists an anonymous claim that holds and is falsifiable.
  \end{itemize}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  The first one is the complementary condition to \cref{th:AHF}.
  It implies the second one.
  The second one implies the first one as $f$ can be extended to $\intvl{\hist} = \intvl{\max H}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}
  \label{th:noindic}
  If $a_1 \ileadstost[f_1] φ$ and $a_2 \ileadstost[f_2] φ'$ with $\ar_1 ≠ \ar_2$ and $f_1(∅) = f_2(∅)$ and $∀ f ∈ \set{f_1, f_2}: \card{{\allleadstoinv}(f)} ∉ ℕ$, then there is no anonymous, falsifiable and indicative about $i_1$ claim that holds (here, considering indicativeness as mandating to know that $P_{i_1} = φ$).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  Any claim that holds must include $\allleadsto$.
  Define $F = \set{f_1, f_2}$.
  
  From indicativeness about $i_1$, $∃ θ ∈ Θ ∩ (\allhists × \set{i_1} × P) ⊆ {\allleadsto} \suchthat ∅ ≠ π(\set{{\allleadstog} \suchthat θ ⊆ {\allleadstog}} ∩ C)(i_1) = φ$.
  Define $H ∈ \incr(\allhists)$ as the query corresponding to $θ$, thus $H = θ(i_1, P)$.

  Define $g ∈ P^{\allhists}$ as $g = \set{(\hist, f_1(\hist)) \suchthat ∃ ∅ ≠ \histp ∈ \intvl{\hist} ∩ H} ∪ \set{(\hist, f_2(\hist)) \suchthat \intvl{\hist} ∩ H ⊆ \set{∅}}$.
  Then, $g ∈ R(F)$. To show this, we need to show that $∀ H' ∈ \finite(\incr(\allhists)): ∃ f ∈ F \suchthat \restr{g}{H'} = \restr{f}{H'}$.

  It follows that $π(\set{{\allleadstog} \suchthat θ ⊆ {\allleadstog}} ∩ C)(i_1) ≠ φ$.

\subsection{Extensions}
  \begin{theorem}
    \label{th:struExt}
    If $C$ is strongly unfalsifiable, then any wider claim $C' ⊇ C$ is strongly unfalsifiable.
  \end{theorem}
  \begin{proof}
    $Θ ⊆ C^⊆ ⊆ {C'}^⊆$.
  \end{proof}

  \begin{theorem}
    \label{th:partlyU}
    If $C = C_1 ∩ C_2$ with $C_1$ strongly unfalsifiable and $C_2$ coverable, then $C$ is unfalsifiable.
  \end{theorem}
  \begin{proof}
    From $C_1$ being strongly unfalsifiable and $C_2$ being coverable, we obtain
    $Θ ∩ C_2^⊆ ⊆ (C_1 ∩ C_2)^⊆$ (\cref{th:extStronglyU}).

    Pick any ${\allleadstog} ∈ C_2$ with ${\allleadstog} ∉ C_1$ (thus ${\allleadstog} ∉ C$) and any $θ ∈ Θ ∩ \powerset{\allleadstog}$,
    and let us show that $θ ∈ C^⊆$.

    From $θ ⊆ {\allleadstog}$ we have $θ ∈ C_2^⊆$.
    Thus, $θ ∈ (C_1 ∩ C_2)^⊆ = C^⊆$.
  \end{proof}
  \begin{theorem}
    \label{th:strongUToStrictU}
    If $C = C_1 ∩ C_2$ with $C_1$ strongly unfalsifiable and $C_2$ falsifiable and coverable, then $C$ is unfalsifiable but not strongly unfalsifiable.
  \end{theorem}
  \begin{proof}
    The claim $C_2$ is falsifiable, thus, is not strongly unfalsifiable.
    Thus, the more precise claim $C$ is not strongly unfalsifiable (\cref{th:struExt}).
    \Cref{th:partlyU} concludes.
  \end{proof}

    Any relation $\allleadstog$ is equivalent to $∪ \set{\restr{\allleadstog}{O} \suchthat O ∈ (\finite(\incr(\allhists))^I)}$.

  \begin{theorem}
    A claim $C$ is falsifiable iff its complement $\Cbar = \allleadstos ∖ C$ satisfies:
    $∀ {\allleadstog} ∈ \Cbar, ∃ θ ⊆ {\allleadstog} \suchthat S_θ ⊆ \Cbar$, or equivalently:
    $∀ {\allleadstog} ∈ \Cbar, ∃ O ∈ (\allhists)^I \suchthat S_{\restr{\allleadstog}{O_O}} ⊆ \Cbar$.
  \end{theorem}
  \begin{proof}
    The first phrasing follows from the definition of falsifiability.
    Also, the second phrasing implies the first one: pick $θ = \restr{\allleadstog}{O_O}$.

    Let us prove that the first phrasing implies the second one.
    Given $θ$, define $O = \set{(i, \argmax_{\hist ∈ (θ(i) ∪ ∅)} \card{\hist})}$ as associating to each $i$ the longest corresponding $\hist$ from $θ$, or the empty sequence if $θ(i)$ is empty.
    It follows that $θ ⊆ \restr{\allleadstog}{O_O}$.
    Thus, $S_{\restr{\allleadstog}{O_O}} ⊆ S_θ$.
    As $S_θ ⊆ \Cbar$, we obtain $S_{\restr{\allleadstog}{O_O}} ⊆ \Cbar$.
  \end{proof}

  \begin{corollary}
    A claim $C$ is falsifiable iff $∀ f ∈ C^{\allpriobss} \suchthat ∀ O, O' ∈ \allpriobss: \restr{f(O)}{O ∩ O'} = \restr{f(O')}{O ∩ O'}$:
    \[∪_{O ∈ \allpriobss} \restr{f(O)}{O} ∈ C.\]
  \end{corollary}
  \begin{proof}
    Consider a falsifiable claim $C$, consider such an $f$, and let us prove that $∪_{O ∈ \allpriobss} \restr{f(O)}{O} ∈ C$.
    Define ${\allleadstog} ⊆ (I × \allhists) × P$ as $∪_{O ∈ \allpriobss} \restr{f(O)}{O}$. Its domain equals $I × \allhists$, and it is right-unique thanks to the condition on $f$.
    Thus, \cref{thm:caractFals} applies and ${\allleadstog} ∈ C$.

    Conversely, assume that the statement about all $f$ holds and let us prove that $C$ is falsifiable.
    Consider any ${\allleadstog} ∈ \allleadstos$ such that $\set{\restr{\allleadstog}{O} \suchthat O ∈ \allpriobss} ⊆ C^⊆$.
    Given any $O$, we have $\restr{\allleadstog}{O} ∈ C^⊆$ and therefore there is some $e_O ∈ C$ such that $\restr{\allleadstog}{O} = \restr{e_O}{O}$.
    Define $f$ as $f(O) = e_O$.
    As this $f$ satisfies the condition in the hypothesis, we obtain that $∪_{O ∈ \allpriobss} \restr{f(O)}{O} ∈ C$, hence, $∪_{O ∈ \allpriobss} \restr{\allleadstog}{O} ∈ C$.
    Therefore, ${\allleadstog} ∈ C$.
    Apply \cref{thm:caractFals} to conclude that $C$ is falsifiable.
  \end{proof}

    A claim $C$ is falsifiable iff $∀ f ∈ \left(C^⊆\right)^{\allpriobss} \suchthat ∀ O, O' ∈ \allpriobss: \restr{f(O)}{O ∩ O'} = \restr{f(O')}{O ∩ O'}$:

  \end{corollary}
  \begin{corollary}
    \label{th:corffalsdom}
    A claim $C$ is falsifiable iff $∀ f ∈ ∪_{O ∈ \allpriobss} \set{\set{O} × (P^O ∩ C^⊆)} \suchthat ∀ O, O' ∈ \allpriobss: \restr{f(O)}{O ∩ O'} = \restr{f(O')}{O ∩ O'}$:
    \[∪_{O ∈ \allpriobss} f(O) ∈ C.\]
  \end{corollary}
  \begin{corollary}
    \label{th:veryunfals}
    Any non-trivial claim $C$ satisfying $Θ ⊆ C^⊆$ is not falsifiable.
  \end{corollary}
  \begin{proof}
    Pick any ${\allleadstog} ∉ C$.
    Per hypothesis, $∀ O ∈ \allpriobss$: $\restr{\allleadstog}{O} ∈ C^⊆$.
    Define $f$ as $∀ O ∈ \allpriobss: f(O) = \restr{\allleadstog}{O}$.
    Observe that $∪_{O ∈ \allpriobss} f(O) = {\allleadstog}$ and hence $∪_{O ∈ \allpriobss} f(O) ∉ C$.
    Apply \cref{th:corffalsdom}.
  \end{proof}

  \begin{corollary}
    A claim $C$ is falsifiable iff $\set{{\allleadstog} ∈ \allleadstos \suchthat ∀ θ ∈ Θ: ({\allleadstog} ∩ θ) ∈ (C^⊆ ∩ Θ)} = C$.
  \end{corollary}

  A relation $\allleadstog$ is obs-compatible with $C$ iff $∀ O ∈ \allpriobss: \restr{\allleadstog}{O} ∈ C^⊆$, equivalently, iff $∀ θ ∈ Θ: ({\allleadstog} ∩ θ) ∈ (C^⊆ ∩ Θ)$.

\begin{remark}
  The question of falsifiability is whether the set of relations $\allleadstog$ “compatible” with $C^⊆ ∩ Θ$ equals $C$.
  Compatibility needs to consider the observables, not a subset of it.
  Define $Θ_1 ⊊ Θ$ as the observations that observe only one pair $(i, \hist)$.
  Define $R_1 = \set{{\allleadstog} ∈ \allleadstos \suchthat ∀ θ ∈ Θ_1: ({\allleadstog} ∩ θ) ∈ (C^⊆ ∩ Θ)}$.
  Note that $R ⊆ R_1$.
  We know that $C$ is falsifiable iff $R ⊆ C$. Thus, $C$ is not falsifiable implies $C ⊊ R ⊆ R_1$ thus $C ⊊ R_1$. But we do not have that $C ⊊ R_1$ implies $C$ not falsifiable: could have $C ⊊ R_1$ but still $R = C$.
\end{remark}
